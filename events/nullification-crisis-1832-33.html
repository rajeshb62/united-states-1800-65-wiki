Nullification Crisis (1832–1833)

The Nullification Crisis was South Carolina’s attempt to declare the protective tariffs of 1828 and 1832 null and void within her limits, and the federal government’s response by proclamation, Force Bill, and compromise. Andrew Jackson answered with “Our Federal Union: It must be preserved”; John C. Calhoun stood as the doctrine’s apostle; Henry Clay’s compromise tariff and the Force Bill closed the winter without battle; Thomas Hart Benton and John Quincy Adams watched the Senate and House fights; Alexander H. Stephens later read the episode as a chapter in the long compact controversy between federation and consolidation.

Jefferson Day toast and the doctrine named

On Jefferson’s birthday (13 April 1830) the doctrine received its public unveiling before the ordinance. A subscription dinner at Washington, ostensibly to honor Jefferson as founder of the political school of the subscribers, issued twenty-four regular toasts that savored of the new doctrine of nullification. Many broke off and refused to attend further; the company remained numerous. When the regular toasts were over, President Jackson gave the volunteer that electrified the country: “Our Federal Union: It must be preserved.” Calhoun’s next toast did not allay suspicion: “The Union: next to our Liberty the most dear” — liberty before union, State rights, inequality of burthens and benefits. Connecting with Robert Y. Hayne’s speech and South Carolina publications, the day revealed nullification as a distinct doctrine with Calhoun for apostle. Benton held that Madison’s Virginia resolutions of 1798–99 never supposed a right in a State, of its own motion, to annul an act of Congress; Madison in retirement recoiled from the South Carolina interpretation and from being made its pedestal.

The tariff of 1828 marked the era from which nullification takes its origin and from which a serious division dates between North and South — the work of politicians and manufacturers begun for woollens and swollen by other interests until all manufacturing interests needed to carry the bill were included. Hayne’s moderation in later tariff debate — sorrow more than anger, loyalty to the Union — had no effect on the protective majority. The presidential canvass of 1832 put the American system in issue; the issue was found against it; South Carolina alone refused to wait for regular extinction of the system under the approaching Congress.

Ordinance and proclamation

On 24 November 1832, a fortnight after the election that decided the tariff’s fate, South Carolina issued her ordinance of nullification. It declared the tariff acts — especially those of 29 May 1828 and 14 July 1832 — unauthorized by the Constitution, null and void, and no law; forbade enforcement of the duties within the State; required the legislature to give the ordinance full effect; cut off appeals to the Supreme Court in related cases; and threatened that any federal act closing her ports or coercing her otherwise than through civil tribunals would absolve her from the Union and authorize a separate government. Ordinance, proclamation, gathering of force, and the political theater in which Webster–Hayne echoes still framed the nature of the Union filled the next weeks.

Jackson answered on 10 December with a proclamation denying the right of a single State to annul an act of Congress, asserting the Union’s perpetuity, and preparing military and naval enforcement. The ordinance reached him in early December. In his annual message the President had hoped reduction of duties after extinction of the public debt would compose the question; when proceedings continued — organization of troops, collection of arms, declarations hostile to the Union — he sent Congress in January the promised communication asking powers the exigency demanded. Adams’s December 1832 diary records Major Hamilton, through J. W. Taylor, bringing the proclamation; George McDuffie jocularly reporting that they had nullified the Tariff Act of last summer; Adams answering that ordinance and proclamation must fight it out. Adams offered a resolution calling for copies of proclamation and ordinance; the House refused consideration 106–64. The message against nullification was later referred to Judiciary under Bell of Tennessee; 25,000 copies ordered printed.

Force Bill, Verplanck, and Clay’s compromise

Gulian C. Verplanck’s bill from Ways and Means promised larger reduction after the election’s verdict against the American system. On 12 February Clay asked leave to introduce his own “compromise” measure, prefacing that whether rightfully or wrongfully the tariff stood in imminent danger and must fall at the next session if not preserved by some means not now visible. Clay’s bill swept like a hurricane: engrossment 105–71, final passage 119–85. The Revenue Collection bill — Force Bill to its friends, Bloody Bill to the nullifiers — was taken up amid disorderly struggle. Wilkins of Pennsylvania, reporting it for Judiciary, insisted it revived previous laws and contained nothing novel but contingent authority to remove the custom-house from one building to another — to put it out of harm’s way after 1 February without using force. Calhoun’s resolutions on the powers of the government restated the compact and each State’s equal right to judge infractions and the mode of redress. Unionist majority and Jackson men’s determination that nullification should not stand as a working remedy even where they spoke state-rights language on other questions carried the Force Bill.

A hollow truce between Clay and Calhoun — early and long rival aspirants, united only against Jackson and Martin Van Buren — underwrote the compromise winter. Years later they quarrelled in open Senate over which had been master of the other in 1833: Calhoun declared he had Clay on his back; Clay retorted he would not own him for the meanest of his slaves. “Compromise legislation” was a popular misnomer without foundation in the Constitution — agreements of mutual consent, not votes binding future Congresses. South Carolina, faced with compromise on the tariff and force in reserve, withdrew the ordinance; the crisis closed without battle.

Constitutional meanings

To Jackson and Benton, nullification was incompatible with a government that could operate on individuals and collect a common revenue. To the Carolina theorists — and later to Stephens — the tariff fight was an instance of compact remedy; Stephens listed no president from Jefferson to Lincoln elected who did not hold the government a compact of sovereign states, explained Jackson’s proclamation, and quoted the farewell’s call to maintain unimpaired the sovereignty of the states, while still separating that language from secession “in disregard of her obligations.” Adams, no Jackson man, nonetheless sat through Force Bill and compromise as a Union measure against a State veto of federal law. The crisis thus remains both a practical settlement (compromise tariff + Force Bill + withdrawal) and an unresolved argument about where paramount authority resides — an argument Stephens would reopen after Appomattox.

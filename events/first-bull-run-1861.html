First Bull Run (1861)

The First Battle of Bull Run (First Manassas), fought on 21 July 1861, was the war’s first great field clash in Virginia. Irvin McDowell advanced from Washington against P. G. T. Beauregard at Manassas; the Union army was driven back; panic filled the roads toward the capital; and George B. McClellan was brought east to rebuild what became the Army of the Potomac. The fight sat between the firing on fort-sumter-1861 and the peninsula-campaign-1862 — the opening eastern proof that the rebellion would not be put down by a single summer parade.

Plans, Anaconda, and the three-months’ clock

Winfield Scott’s “Anaconda” plan and the need to use the 75,000 three-months’ militia before enlistments expired framed the July advance. Primary objects — capital safe, Baltimore open, Ohio line protected — had been substantially achieved in the first weeks; where to march and when to fight rose again with imperative demand. Scott did not intend the short-term militia to idle while three-years’ armies perfected organization for a fall and winter campaign. Arlington Heights, Harper’s Ferry, and pressure toward Manassas filled the interim programme. West Virginia’s mountain division of Virginia already showed that Alleghany geography could split a State; Bull Run would test whether a raw federal column could break the Confederate military league gathering south of the Potomac.

McDowell’s advance

It had been arranged that McDowell’s advance against the enemy at Manassas should begin 9 July; by extraordinary exertions he was ready and issued marching orders on 16 July. Organization was very imperfect and preparations far from complete. Many regiments reached him but two days before, some only on the day he moved. He started with barely wagons enough for ammunition and hospital supplies; tents, baggage, and rations were to follow. Utmost caution was enjoined to avoid another Vienna or Big Bethel disaster. Three things his marching orders held unpardonable: to come upon a battery or breastwork without knowledge of its position; to be surprised; to fall back. The army was a new, untried machine; men unused to fatigues and privations of a march made progress slow. With a cumbersome movement it felt its way toward Fairfax Court House and Centreville, enemy outposts retiring with time enough to spare.

Tyler commanded the first division (four brigades); Hunter the second (two); Heintzelman the third (three); Miles the fifth (two). Runyon’s fourth division stayed to guard communications. Aggregate strength about 34,320; marching column proper a little less than 28,000 including artillery — forty-nine guns — and a single battalion of cavalry.

Bull Run line and Blackburn’s Ford

On the morning of 18 July Tyler reached Centreville and found the enemy everywhere retired behind Bull Run — a winding, sluggish stream flowing southeasterly toward the Potomac, about thirty-two miles southwest of Washington, fordable in many places but often with steep, sometimes precipitous and rocky banks and wooded heights on the west. Three miles beyond lay Manassas Junction on a high open plateau where the railroads from Richmond and the Shenandoah Valley meet. Slight field-works with fourteen or fifteen heavy guns and about 2,000 men protected the junction. Beauregard, in command since 1 June, had gathered nearly 22,000 men and twenty-nine guns; Holmes from Aquia Creek raised the force above 23,000 and thirty-five guns. Instead of holding only the earthworks, Beauregard posted the army close down to Bull Run along some seven miles from the Manassas railroad to the stone bridge on the Warrenton turnpike, guarding the intermediate fords.

Tyler, ordered to observe roads but not bring on an engagement, followed toward Blackburn’s Ford near the center of Beauregard’s line. Lured by easy approach, reconnaissance became skirmish and skirmish a preliminary battle: sixty men fallen, two field-pieces extricated with difficulty, one regiment retreated in confusion, three others deployed for a new charge before McDowell’s restraint closed the day. The lesson of imperfect organization and over-eager advance was written before the main battle.

Battle, reverse, and panic

The main action of 21 July turned McDowell’s flanking movement against the Confederate left into a hard fight on the plateaus and fords west of Bull Run. Joseph E. Johnston’s Valley troops, arriving by the Manassas Gap Railroad, stiffened Beauregard at the decisive hours — the junction’s strategic meaning made real when two Confederate armies met on the same field. Union partial successes of the morning gave way to afternoon reverse; the new machine broke under counterattack and the disordered retreat toward Washington became panic on the roads among soldiers and civilian spectators who had come out as to a spectacle. Ninety-day men and early three-year volunteers mixed under imperfect staff work; ammunition wagons and hospital trains could not steady a column that had been forbidden to fall back and then fell back in confusion. The meaning for Lincoln’s Administration was plain: three-months’ war was over; a long war of three-years’ armies had begun. Congress in special session, already meeting under the April call, faced a war of years rather than a summer’s correction of rebellion.

Aftermath: McClellan and the Army of the Potomac

On the day after the battle McClellan was ordered to Washington. He arrived 26 July and next day assumed command of the division of the Potomac — troops in and around Washington on both sides of the river. His later report recalled about 50,000 infantry, less than 1,000 cavalry, and 650 artillerymen with nine imperfect field batteries of thirty pieces when he took charge. Tens of thousands of infantry gathered for the long Virginia war. Special session of Congress, the contraband question, Frémont in the West, and organization of the Army of the Potomac filled the weeks that followed. European neutrality already disappointed Northern expectation that England and France would favor the Union; Bull Run’s reverse deepened the need for a disciplined eastern army before foreign recognition questions hardened. Bull Run thus stands between Sumter’s uprising and the Peninsula bid of 1862 — the first eastern field where raw courage met imperfect organization and found that courage alone did not take Manassas.

Chronology

  • May–Jun 1861: Call to arms; Anaconda; three-months’ clock; West Virginia rising.
  • 16 Jul 1861: McDowell’s marching orders.
  • 18 Jul: Centreville; Blackburn’s Ford reconnaissance-skirmish.
  • 21 Jul: Battle of Bull Run / First Manassas; Union reverse and retreat.
  • 26–27 Jul: McClellan arrives and assumes command about Washington.

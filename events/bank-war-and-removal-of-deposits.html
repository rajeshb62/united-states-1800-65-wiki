Bank War and Removal of the Deposits (1832–1836)

The Bank War was the contest between President Andrew Jackson and the Second Bank of the United States — Nicholas Biddle’s corporation at Philadelphia — over recharter, the custody of the public deposits, and the place of a great moneyed institution in a federal republic. It runs from House investigation and the veto of recharter through the election of 1832, the removal of the deposits into state banks, the commercial pressure remembered as the panic of 1834, the Senate’s censure of the President, Jackson’s protest, and the speculative aftermath that prepared the panic-of-1837. Thomas Hart Benton led the Senate Jacksonians; the House fought the same war through investigations and votes; the monetary weather that followed is theorized on credit-and-confidence-carey.

Investigation, recharter, and veto

The Twenty-Second Congress was the session at which the great contest for renewal of the charter was brought on and decided. The bank leaders meant to carry the charter quietly through Senate and then House; the opposition determined to attack incessantly, display the evil of the institution, rouse the people, and prepare them to sustain the veto. Benton conceived a House investigation under the charter’s own provision: if denied, guilt shrinking from detection; if admitted, misconduct would be found. The House was preferred as the grand inquest of the nation, and so that a contest might go on there while the Senate passed the charter. Amendments and inquiries in the Senate forced the bank into defences and side-blows. The recharter bill passed both Houses; Jackson vetoed it.

The veto sat in the campaign of 1832: Anti-Masonic side-show, nullification running in parallel, and the President’s re-election as a popular judgment against the Bank. Jacksonians treated the veto as constitutional and democratic necessity — the Bank a monopoly dangerous to liberty and to the equal rights of the States. In the House, Bank and manufactures debates became the ground on which parties were realigning after Clay’s compromise and the Force Bill winter. Effects of the veto and later fights over the three per cents, illegal currency, and sale of the United States stock in the national bank filled the sequel years.

Removal of the deposits: Duane and Taney

From the moment of the removal it was seen that the Bank’s plan was to force return of the deposits and renewal of the charter by operating on the business of the country and the alarms of the people. Loans and accommodations were to cease at the mother bank and branches and at local banks under its control; discounts stopped while curtailments called business men for payment of all they owed; pressure was made to fall on large establishments employing many operatives so as to throw laboring people out of employment. In September 1833 Jackson determined to withdraw the deposits of public moneys from the Bank and place them in sundry State banks. A paper was read to the Cabinet on the eighteenth of the month; William J. Duane, refusing to execute the policy, was dismissed; Roger B. Taney became Secretary of the Treasury. Jacksonians defended the removal as reclaiming the people’s money from a hostile corporation; the “pet banks,” the congressional reaction, and the commercial pressure of 1834 followed. Biddle assured allies that the Bank was prepared for the crisis; some House members resolved to wait upon the evidence before declaring for or against the institution. Huntington of Connecticut anticipated a bank committee that would report approving the removal; Richard Rush’s intrusion into the deposit controversy hurt Anti-Masonry in Pennsylvania. Taney’s report to Congress on the removal became the official Treasury narrative.

Panic of 1834, censure, and protest

Commercial distress paired with the rise of the Whig party and the Senate’s censure of Jackson. In April 1834 the Senate resolved to censure the President for his interferences with the public revenues. The President sent a protest and demanded that it be entered on the Senate journals; Poindexter moved that it should not be received; Clay, Webster, and Preston were absent when the excitement peaked. In the House, Wise and Baylie Peyton offered competing resolutions on custody of the public moneys and on whether the Senate’s censure had infringed the prerogatives of the House. Ways and Means under Polk, deposit-bank investigations, and appropriation conferences filled the session. The Senate’s attempt to brand an executive act as a usurpation met Jackson’s refusal to accept the brand. Years on, the censure was struck from the journals — Jackson’s “crowning mercy.”

Speculation toward 1837

The deposit system and surplus distribution fed a boom that broke when confidence failed. One contemporary reading compared the federal surplus of the mid-1830s to a great estate forcing mortgage payments into banks or into court in gold — swelling apparent banking profits until the currency snapped. High credit, on that showing, follows security of person and property and density of population; American disorder of 1836–7 was explained by surplus revenue and currency operations rather than by free banking itself (full argument on credit-and-confidence-carey). The Bank War proper ends with the federal charter’s expiration and Biddle’s turn to a Pennsylvania charter; its political legacy is the Democratic identification with hard money and against a national bank, and the Whig identification with recharter — the ground of Van Buren’s Independent Treasury fight and of the Log-Cabin campaign of 1840.

Chronology

  • 1832: House investigation; recharter passes; Jackson vetoes; re-elected.
  • Sep 1833: Deposit removal ordered; Duane dismissed; Taney at Treasury.
  • 1834: Panic of 1834; Senate censure; presidential protest.
  • 1836–37: Speculative surplus and credit disorder → Panic of 1837.

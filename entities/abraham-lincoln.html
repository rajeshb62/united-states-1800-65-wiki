Abraham Lincoln

Abraham Lincoln (1809–1865) — Illinois lawyer, Republican nominee of 1860, and sixteenth President — was the Union executive of secession winter, Sumter, emancipation, and Appomattox week. The life runs from frontier foundations through the debates, the secret journey to Washington, the war Cabinet, and the night of 14 April. At Petersburg and Richmond he met Grant; the 1860 letters and the Hampton Roads table preserve the Confederate side of peace talk. The Lincoln who emerges is not a cradle-to-grave romance, but the attested wartime President who held the forts, timed the proclamation, and met peace commissioners before the armies closed the war.

Frontier, legislature, and the Stone protest

The pioneer Lincolns open the story: the grandfather’s Kentucky settlement from Virginia, Thomas Lincoln’s removal to Indiana when slaveholding Kentucky had already drawn class lines, and the family’s Illinois arrival in time for the “winter of the deep snow” of 1830–31. In New Salem the young Lincoln flatboated to New Orleans for Denton Offutt, clerked when Offutt’s ventures failed, and in the summer of 1832 answered Governor Reynolds’s call against Black Hawk — elected captain of his company, reënlisting when the first term expired, and returning to New Salem only ten days before the August election for which he had already announced by handbill. That canvass was his only popular defeat; New Salem itself gave him nearly every vote. Two years later Sangamon sent him to the legislature. Poverty and debt still clung, but the pioneer portion of the life was closed: Springfield, the lodging of Joshua F. Speed, law study, and the long Whig friendships of Stuart, Logan, and Butler followed.

On 3 March 1837, the last day before the Vandalia session adjourned, Lincoln and Dan Stone caused to be entered on the journal a protest against the assembly’s pro-slavery resolutions. Slavery, they recorded, was founded on “both injustice and bad policy”; abolition agitation they thought likely to increase the evils; Congress had no power to interfere in the States, yet possessed power over the District of Columbia which ought not to be exercised without the District’s request. The paper stands as the authentic beginning of a momentous career. Marriage to Mary Todd, the Shields duel farce, partnership with Logan, and the circuit years filled the interval before national office; the brief autobiography written later for Jesse Fell compressed the quiet that followed Congress: from 1849 to 1854 he practiced law “more assiduously than ever before,” “losing interest in politics” until the repeal of the Missouri Compromise aroused him again.

Congress and the Mexican War

The Thirtieth Congress organized on 6 December 1847 with Lincoln the sole Whig from Illinois and among the least known names on the roll. The House soon attached George Ashmun’s amendment declaring that the war with Mexico had been “unnecessarily and unconstitutionally commenced by the President.” Lincoln’s floor speech held that Whig opposition meant exactly that judgment — marching an army into a peaceful Mexican settlement was a “naked, impudent absurdity” — while insisting that once the war was the nation’s cause, Whigs voted supplies and gave blood. Letters to Herndon defended the anti-war votes by pointing to Whig soldiers who themselves denounced the war’s unjust beginning. The single congressional term made him better known than before and raised his value on the circuit; it did not yet make him a national leader. That awaited Kansas–Nebraska.

Kansas–Nebraska, debates, and Freeport

Douglas’s Nebraska bill and the repeal of the Missouri Compromise remade Northern politics. Border Ruffians, the bogus Kansas legislature elected by thousands of Missouri ballots against a few hundred legal votes, the Topeka constitution, and civil war in the Territory followed. Then came Dred Scott (announced two days after Buchanan’s inauguration), Lecompton, and Douglas’s revolt against that constitution — then the Illinois senatorial canvass of 1858 that for a season eclipsed national issues.

In the joint debates Lincoln forced at Freeport the question whether a territorial legislature could exclude slavery before statehood. Republican managers at Mendota had urged him not to ask it. His answer was that he was “killing larger game”: if Douglas answered, he could never be President, and “the battle of 1860 is worth a hundred of this.” Douglas’s Freeport reply — that the people could exclude slavery by unfriendly legislation — became a Southern weapon against him two years later. Ohio speeches in 1859 kept the Republican cause with its “undoubted friends” against any Greeley temptation to adopt Douglas. Harper’s Ferry closed the sensational decade; Lincoln was not its author, but its political weather entered the canvass of 1860.

Cooper Institute, Chicago, and election

Among autumn invitations of 1859 was a Brooklyn lecture; Lincoln accepted for February provided they would take a political speech. On arrival in New York he learned the place was Cooper Institute. William Cullen Bryant presided; David Dudley Field escorted him; Greeley and the city’s political culture filled the hall. The Tribune placed the address since Clay and Webster beside any assemblage of New York intellect. Cooper Institute stood as the capstone of Lincoln’s second intellectual period — the decade of speech-making that ended just before the nominations of 1860.

Charleston’s Democratic convention broke on the two-thirds rule and Southern withdrawal; Baltimore nominations followed for the Douglas remnant while Breckinridge and Bell tickets completed a four-party field. The Republican National Convention met at Chicago on 16 May 1860 and named Lincoln of Illinois and Hannibal Hamlin of Maine. The canvass that followed made him President-elect while Governor Gist’s South Carolina circular and Charleston preparations — “from the ballot to the bullet” — were already under way. Anderson at Moultrie, Buchanan’s message and truces, the Committees of Thirteen and Thirty-three, and the forty muskets episode closed the inherited fort crisis Buchanan bequeathed and Lincoln faced.

President-elect, secret journey, inaugural, and Cabinet

Salmon P. Chase’s congratulation of 7 November 1860 fixed the tone: “The lead is yours. The responsibility is great.” Through the winter Lincoln answered timid Republicans and Southern Unionists who begged public statements to quiet the South, while South Carolina’s convention, Anderson’s Christmas transfer from Moultrie to Sumter, the Star of the West, Montgomery’s Confederacy, and failed compromise filled the newspapers. Formal invitations from Northern legislatures marked the road to the 4 March inauguration. After a public progress eastward, morning telegrams forced abandonment of the announced programme: a quick secret night passage through Baltimore on 23 February. Open travel through Baltimore would almost certainly have meant assassination; the President-elect was “smuggled into the capital.”

Installed at Willard’s with little more than a week to prepare, he visited Buchanan’s Cabinet, Congress, and the Court. Cabinet framing, by his own later account, had begun on election night at the Springfield telegraph office. Seward’s bid to be “premier or president” — amendments to the inaugural, a withdrawal letter, Lincoln’s reply of 5 March withdrawing Seward’s letter of the 2nd — tested whether the new President or his Secretary of State would govern. On 5 March the Senate received the nominations: Seward at State, Chase at Treasury, Cameron at War, Welles at Navy, Smith at Interior, Bates Attorney General, Montgomery Blair Postmaster General — a representative combination of experience, integrity, sections, and parties. Inductions followed on the 6th; the first Cabinet meeting that evening opened the Sumter struggle.

Sumter, call to arms, border states, and Frémont

Lincoln’s Sumter policy was to “send bread to Anderson”: if the rebels fired on bread, they could not convince the world he began civil war. Open notice went to Governor Pickens; the Fox relief expedition and the Meigs–Pickens movement were ordered to sail nearly together in early April. Sumter was fired on 11 April and fell within days. The Southern compact reading of the same crisis held South Carolina’s right to the fort after secession and Northern “breach of faith”; Lincoln’s proclamation of 15 April, in that reading, inaugurated the war.

The first call under the Act of 1795 asked seventy-five thousand three-months militia and convened Congress for 4 July. Border-state strategy — Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri held by loyal majorities and military energy; Virginia’s secret ordinance and Stephens’s Richmond mission of 22 April — filled the spring. Butler’s early Maryland offer to suppress rumored slave insurrection marked how quickly slavery entered war logistics. At St. Louis, German population and Francis P. Blair’s leadership contested Governor Jackson’s secession course.

Frémont’s military emancipation order of 30 August 1861 surprised the country. Lincoln first asked modification to conform to the Confiscation Act of 6 August; on 11 September he required that conformity by open order. A private letter to O. H. Browning (22 September) called Frémont’s proclamation “purely political,” not military necessity, and warned that permanent freeing of slaves by a general would be “dictatorship” — a matter for lawmakers. On 24 October Frémont was relieved (Hunter temporarily in command), the order to wait if Frémont were in battle. The incident made Frémont an antislavery rallying cry; Lincoln added nothing publicly. McClellan, ordered to Washington the day after Bull Run, took the Army of the Potomac at the end of July.

1862: Trent, Stanton, and Peninsula pressure

The Trent affair diverted public impatience with army inaction into a threatened war with Britain over seized Confederate envoys; then came the Cameron–Stanton succession at the War Department as the conflict’s unintended destruction of slavery deepened. An autograph campaign memorandum from Lincoln to McClellan about 1 December 1861 — later judged “eminently wise and sagacious” — returned after ten days with a pencil note showing little impression. Through the Peninsula the administration meant McDowell’s corps to reinforce when Virginia permitted; Franklin’s division reached Yorktown in time yet went unused until after evacuation. Fair Oaks and the Seven Days produced McClellan’s hallucination that the enemy doubled his numbers and the extraordinary Savage’s Station complaint to Stanton of 28 June. The President did everything in his power to satisfy the general’s unreasonable demands. The annual message of December 1861 had already offered compensated abolishment; the Monitor–Merrimac morning of 9 March 1862 carried gloom and then relief through the Executive Mansion. Emancipation policy as yet remained with Congress and the border states — until Hunter and July forced Lincoln’s own hand.

Emancipation: July Cabinet, Antietam, final edict, Black soldiers

Hunter’s military emancipation order of 9 May 1862 reached Lincoln through the newspapers while he was still pressing compensated abolishment on the border. On 22 July, after full Cabinet discussion, he read a draft proclaiming freedom for slaves in rebellious states on 1 January 1863 — a decision ahead of the entire Cabinet, with only two giving unreserved concurrence — and postponed publication until a victory. Chase noted Negro regiments discussed; Lincoln held general arming premature but allowed defensive arming of slaves within the lines.

Antietam supplied the hinge. Lincoln had handed McClellan at Washington an aggregate of more than a hundred thousand; McClellan reported some eighty-seven thousand at the creek. After the battle the preliminary proclamation of 22 September 1862 followed; the final edict of 1 January 1863 made military emancipation the war’s central measure. The annual message of 1 December 1862 had already stated the premise: “Without slavery, the rebellion could never have existed; without slavery it could not continue.” Emancipation was coupled from the first with the expectation of Negro soldiers; Davis answered the July labor order and the arming of Black troops with retaliation claims under the laws of war. McClellan’s post-Antietam delays ended in November with Burnside’s reluctant succession. French mediation overtures were declined; West Virginia’s admission, colonization schemes in the Clay tradition, Missouri’s factional plague, and the Seward–Chase Cabinet poles filled the same season’s side chapters. East Tennessee remained “dearer to the heart of the President than any other” objective.

1863: draft, Seymour, Vallandigham, Gettysburg Address

The armies had grown from some seventeen thousand at inauguration toward a million. Enrollment and draft under the new statutes brought Governor Seymour of New York into open opposition: after the July riots he sent Samuel J. Tilden and others to urge suspending the draft; telegrams and letters filled the correspondence. Burnside, taking the Department of the Ohio on 25 March, issued Order No. 38 against anti-war manifestations; Vallandigham’s speeches led to arrest, military trial, and a journey toward Canada, while Ohio Democrats nominated him for governor — a Copperhead climax Lincoln’s administration had to live with even as it managed the armies.

On 28 January Lincoln’s frank letter placing Hooker at the head of the Army of the Potomac warned that there were “some things in regard to which I am not quite satisfied with you,” while affirming belief in Hooker’s bravery and skill. After Chancellorsville Hooker telegraphed on 6 May that he saw no way to give general battle with desirable success; Meade assumed command on 28 June. Gettysburg and Vicksburg’s fall closed the first days of July. The cemetery dedication followed in November: Governor Curtin’s project, seventeen loyal states cooperating, David Wills inviting Lincoln on 2 November to give “few appropriate remarks” after Edward Everett’s oration. Lincoln traveled by special train on the 18th with Seward; that evening’s serenade heard Seward thank God the strife would end in removing the evil that deliberation ought to have removed peacefully. On 19 November 1863, after Everett’s long oration, Lincoln spoke the Address that begins “four score and seven years ago.” Everett’s later note and Lincoln’s modest reply close the chapter.

The political war continued: habeas corpus and Bates’s opinion on presidential arrest power; East Tennessee lavished with orders and entreaties; Missouri Claybanks and Charcoals (Conservatives and Radicals) plaguing the White House for nearly two years; the Pomeroy circular and Chase candidacy by late 1863; free-state movements in Arkansas, Louisiana, Tennessee, Maryland, and Missouri under military governors; Grant’s elevation toward general-in-chief as 1864 opened.

1864: renomination, Wade–Davis, peace missions, Atlanta, reelection

Grant’s instructions to Sherman — move against Johnston, break him up, get into the interior — paired with the Rapidan crossing as coordinated strategy. By the melting of the snows nothing short of death could have prevented Lincoln’s renomination at Baltimore on 7 June; Cleveland’s Frémont faction and Missouri dissenters were the visible exceptions. Chase’s bitterness after his failed presidential bid made joint public service impossible until the almost comic collapse of his boom. Reconstruction policy, however, split the party more sharply than personal rivalry.

The message and proclamation of 8 December 1863 had offered pardon on oath to support Constitution and Union and to abide by wartime acts and proclamations. In July 1864 Congress passed a reconstruction bill; Lincoln pocket-vetoed it and on 8 July explained that he declined an inflexible exclusive plan. Chase recorded the “pocketed” bill as condemnation of amnesty policy; August brought the Wade–Davis manifesto’s vigorous attack. Summer peace missions — Greeley’s Niagara pressure through the Tribune, the unofficial Jaquess–Gilmore errand — Lincoln handled so that military movements were not delayed. The Democratic convention at Chicago postponed in hope of accidents and then declared the war a failure; on 31 August, in the same hours of Chicago rejoicing, Hood evacuated Atlanta and Sherman’s entry became bane and antidote. Early’s raid on Washington, Sheridan in the Shenandoah, Cabinet harmony demanded by the Baltimore platform, Taney’s death and Chase’s elevation to Chief Justice, and Sherman’s rest at Atlanta before the March to the Sea completed the reelection year. Lincoln won a second term; the Union’s political as well as military war had turned.

Thirteenth Amendment, Hampton Roads, Richmond, 14 April

The endgame opened with the Thirteenth Amendment’s path through the Thirty-Seventh Congress’s prior measures — District abolition, territories, fugitive law, confiscation — and the proclamations of 22 September 1862 and 1 January 1863. House passage on 31 January 1865 brought an outburst of enthusiasm and a hundred-gun salute; Lincoln signed on 1 February (the Senate later holding presidential approval unnecessary). Maryland, New York, and West Virginia were among the early ratifiers.

Blair’s Mexican–Monroe Doctrine feeler and Lincoln’s 6 December message set the stage for Hampton Roads. On 31 January Seward was instructed to confer at Fort Monroe with Stephens, Hunter, and Campbell on the basis of Lincoln’s 18 January letter to F. P. Blair: restoration of national authority everywhere; no receding on slavery from recent documents; no cessation of hostilities short of the war’s end and the disbanding of hostile forces. Grant retained the commissioners at City Point without discussing their mission, telegraphed Washington, and received Lincoln’s order of 1 February: let nothing change, hinder, or delay military movements. On 3 February, aboard the River Queen, Lincoln and Seward sat four hours with the three Confederates. No official minutes; the commissioners’ own accounts preserve the table. Lincoln’s sine qua non was restoration of the Union; Blair, he said, had acted “without the least authority.” Davis’s and Lincoln’s messages from the opposite chairs survive; Davis’s wrathful public temper afterward contrasted with Lincoln’s almost reverse conviction that rebellion was waning and that generous conciliation might shorten the war — the temper of the second inaugural that February.

Richmond fell the night of 2 April as Davis fled toward Danville. Lincoln entered the captured capital on 4 April, some thirty-six hours after Davis left: transferred to Admiral Porter’s barge with a handful of sailors, walking about a mile and a half on foot among contrabands — never, it was said, in the history of the world did the head of a nation and conqueror of a great rebellion enter the chief city in such humbleness. Grant had entered Petersburg with Meade on the 3rd; Lincoln’s visit, the Weitzel order about a body “calling itself” the Virginia legislature (Stanton countermanding), and the shock of assassination after the week of victory followed. Lee surrendered on the 9th. On Good Friday, 14 April, with “deep and tranquil happiness” after the week’s tidings, Booth shot Lincoln; Payne mangled Seward’s household the same night. The nation learned at breakfast on the 15th. Dying in the moment of a stupendous victory, success’s halo enveloped his memory as worldwide grief answered the telegraph.

Lincoln remains the Union President of the Civil War cluster — the man of Stone’s protest, Freeport’s larger game, bread to Anderson, timed emancipation, Wade–Davis defiance, Hampton Roads firmness, and the Richmond walk.

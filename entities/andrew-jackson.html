Andrew Jackson

Andrew Jackson (1767–1845) — seventh President of the United States — was the executive center of the contests that remade federal power between 1828 and 1837: the Bank War, nullification, removal of the deposits, the Specie Circular, and the farewell that closed his public life. Thomas Hart Benton stood as the General’s Senate lieutenant through those years; John Quincy Adams watched the New England tour, the deposit order, and the Senate censure from the opposition benches. The disorders of Jackson’s last years also read as a crisis of surplus revenue and currency operations rather than a simple morality play about banks. This page is the Jackson of proclamation, veto, protest, and expunging — the President who said the Federal Union must be preserved and who struck at the Bank of the United States as a monopoly dangerous to the Republic.

New Orleans fame as remembered

Before the presidential canvass, Jackson’s public legend was already military. New Orleans stood as the “crowning mercy” of the General’s military life — the parallel later drawn when the Senate expunged the censure of 1834. That fame was the background against which the anti-caucus movement and the 1828 triumph made sense: the victor of 1815 as the people’s candidate against caucus and coalition. This page does not reopen the battle narrative; it carries the fame forward as political capital — Old Hickory as the organizing center of Democratic power once the House election of 1825 had taught his friends that electoral colleges and bargain charges would decide the next canvass.

From House election to the presidency, 1824–28

The old Republican party broke; Jackson’s nomination, the anti-caucus movement, and the House election of 1825 that chose Adams followed. Of the electoral votes Jackson had led; of the States in the House he took seven against Adams’s thirteen and Crawford’s four. Jackson’s friends circulated the charge of a corrupt bargain when Clay entered Adams’s cabinet; the campaign of 1828 made that charge a people’s slogan. His elevation read as the restoration of popular government against caucus and coalition. The close was bitter — Jackson and Calhoun declared elected 11 February 1829 — and after the inauguration the new President’s New England progress of 1833 included a Harvard doctorate Adams thought a cheap and sycophantic honor, four-fifths of the reported sickness “trickery,” and a tour cut short toward Washington. The year 1828 marked the triumph of Jacksonian democracy; beside it ran the tariff of 1828, Calhoun’s Exposition, and the Georgia Indian controversy that would run beside nullification and Bank.

Jefferson Birthday toast and Calhoun

The public legend of the Jefferson Birthday dinner toast — “Our Federal Union: it must be preserved” — stands as the emblem of Jackson’s Unionism in the struggle with Calhoun’s Carolina doctrine. Nullifiers sought to make Jefferson “the pedestal of this colossal heresy,” and Virginia’s legislature almost unanimously repulsed the imputation that the Resolutions of 1798–99 supported South Carolina’s remedy. The nullifiers killed the birthday celebrations themselves: instead of renewal in all time, they were never heard of again. The rupture between President Jackson and Vice-President Calhoun — personal and political — hardened into open enmity under nullification and the Eaton court quarrel. The toast paired with the Webster–Hayne debate and the nature of the Union as the people then argued it.

Nullification: proclamation and force

In 1832–33 South Carolina’s ordinance of nullification tested the Union. Jackson’s proclamation, preparation for armed enforcement, the Force Bill, Clay’s compromise tariff, and the withdrawal of the ordinance followed. From the Senate floor Benton and others received the ordinance, watched the proclamation issue, acknowledged state-rights language, and refused Carolina’s remedy. The proclamation’s close put the momentous case before the people: whether the sacred Union would be preserved and its blessings perpetuated as one people. In the House, Force Bill and compromise filled the Twenty-Second and Twenty-Third Congresses, with Jackson’s proclamation against South Carolina nullification and Clay’s tariff settlement as the winter’s business. Jackson met nullification as rebellion against the laws of the Union, not as a debating society over tariff schedules alone.

Bank veto, 1832

Jackson’s veto of the Bank recharter is the other half of his presidential identity. Friends of the blow against Biddle’s corporation held that when posterity has recounted all the great and good deeds of a long life and the circumstances under which the message was signed, the conclusion will be that in this act he risked more for the happiness and liberties of his fellow-men than in any other. The veto ran through the election of 1832 — Anti-Masonry in the field, nullification overlapping the canvass, Jackson’s re-election as popular ratification of the Bank’s doom. The veto message treated the Bank as a monopoly dangerous to republican government, unconstitutional in spirit if not in every lawyer’s brief, and a foreign-influenced power over the currency. Investigation committees, the three-per-cent debt, sale of United States stock, and the eventual downfall of the federal charter carried the struggle on.

Deposits: Duane, Taney, censure, protest, expunging

Removal of the deposits into state (“pet”) banks opened the second Bank war. Benton was in the private secretary’s orbit when the decision came and repaired to Washington determined to stand by the President, which he believed to be standing by the country. September 1833 brought the Cabinet paper, William J. Duane’s dismissal, Roger B. Taney’s appointment to the Treasury; Adams paused — neither for nor against the Bank until the evidence went to Congress. Congressional reaction, the rise of the Whig name, and the Senate’s censure of Jackson for interfering with the public revenues followed into the panic of 1834.

Jackson’s protest demanded entry on the Senate journal. Benton argued that if the Senate had tried an impeachment in due form, impugning its justice might have been breach of privilege — but the Senate had no privilege to vote an impeachable matter without regular impeachment, and therefore no privilege against the protest. Clay, Webster, and the absentees filled the opposition benches; Wise and Baylie Peyton offered competing House resolutions on custody of the moneys and on whether Senate censure infringed House prerogatives. The expunging resolution became Benton’s multi-year labor — Alabama resolutions, rejections and renewals, perorations, and at last the night when the resolution passed and was executed on the journal. Jackson’s gratification was extreme: too weak to sit at table, he only met the company of expungers and their wives, placed the “head-expunger” in his chair, and withdrew to his sick chamber. That expurgation was called the crowning mercy of Jackson’s civil life, as New Orleans had been of his military.

Specie Circular, surplus, farewell

The monetary agitation of 1836–7 read the disorders of Jackson’s last years as parallel to an estate forced into banks or into court in gold — the federal surplus of the mid-1830s raising apparent banking profits while altering the currency. Distribution of surplus revenue, the land boom, and the Specie Circular that required hard money at the land offices closed the term. Benton was in Donelson’s office when the President’s decision came and drew the rough draught: a second edition of the deposits scene, an immense sensation among disappointed speculators who cried insult to Congress and defiance of cabinet and banks — vindicated when tens of millions of bank paper bound for the land offices were turned back. Jackson’s farewell address warned of sectional danger with a pain likened to Madison’s; what has since taken place showed the apprehensions not groundless.

Retirement and death

Private affections survived Rachel’s death at the moment of the first election — the adopted son and heir at the Hermitage, Donelson as private secretary, Mrs. Donelson presiding in the Presidential mansion — and every just petition or charitable request that had lost in her death the surest channel to the President’s ear and heart. The speculative boom Van Buren inherited and the continuation of anti-slavery agitation closed the General’s public term. For this wiki Jackson remains the attested executive of Bank and nullification, deposits and expunging, Circular and farewell.

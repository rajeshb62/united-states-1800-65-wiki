Henry Clay

Henry Clay (1777–1852) — Speaker, Kentucky senator, Secretary of State, and perennial presidential aspirant — was the Western architect of war, compromise, and the protective “American System.” Contemporaries credited him (with Monroe in the Cabinet) with making the declaration of 1812 obtainable in Congress; at Ghent he fought for Mississippi equivalents against British fisheries claims; later Adams took Clay’s House support in 1825 and made him Secretary of State — the act that Jackson’s friends named a corrupt bargain. That charge became a people’s slogan; Clay’s tariff years run in a sequence from 1816 through the compromise of 1833. The last great act was the Omnibus of 1850, its Senate defeat, and the separate-bills sequel Clay did not live to dominate.

War Speaker and Ghent

Except for the exertions of two men — Monroe in the Cabinet and Clay in Congress — contemporaries held that the declaration of 1812 could not have been obtained. The war was fought under broken finances after the First Bank’s expiration, closed honorably, and made a proud era: Clay’s early identity is already that of the War Hawk Speaker who later appears in 1814 laying Edwards’s A.B. charges before the House.

At Ghent he sat among the American commissioners with Adams, Gallatin, Bayard, and Russell. August 1814 brought the British commissioners, the American refusal to meet on British terms of lodging as a pretension of superiority, and the exchange of powers at the Hôtel des Pays-Bas. Negotiation turned on Indian trade, Mississippi navigation, and fisheries: Clay pressed Western and Mississippi interests against any sacrifice for Atlantic fisheries stipulations; Gallatin sided with Adams on navigation as equivalent. The treaty was signed on Christmas Eve 1814; Christmas Day sealed copies under agreed secrecy; Adams left Ghent in January 1815 after more than seven months he called the most memorable period of his life. The Clay of Ghent is already the Western bargain-maker — not yet the presidential candidate, but already the man who would sell compromise as statesmanship.

American System and the tariff of 1824

The successive tariffs of 1816, 1824, 1828, and the compromise of 1833 form one commercial-policy arc: protection places “loom and anvil beside plough and harrow,” tested by consumption over long periods. The act of 1816 appears as a planters’ and farmers’ measure; 1828 as the first really protective tariff after the low-duty years. For the session of 1823–24 the revision’s object was the protection of home industry — the “American System” — with protection the object and revenue the incident, reversing earlier practice. Webster led New England opposition; Van Buren warned manufacturers not to tie themselves to one party; Monroe approved the bill without denying its revenue character. Clay’s name thereafter attached to the programme. By 1828 the high tariff stood as the origin of nullification doctrine — woolens expanded by log-rolling into lead, hemp, and the rest — and Democratic members who voted it intended to repudiate the system after Jackson’s victory. In 1832 Clay still told the Senate that the protective system was an “overruling necessity” for other sections and that the danger was abandonment, not continuation.

1824–25 and the Secretaryship

The electoral college of 1824 gave Jackson 99, Adams 84, Crawford 41, Clay 37 — no majority of 131. Clay, fourth and out of the House ballot, led Kentucky and other Western friends to Adams; on 9 February 1825 the House chose Adams on a single ballot, thirteen States to Jackson’s seven and Crawford’s four. Clay’s friends (Letcher among them) sounded Adams; Adams himself walked “Incedo super ignes”; the offer of the Department of State came on the 10th for “talents and services” and Western confidence; Webster’s committee notified the President-elect. Senate confirmation of Clay as Secretary drew twenty-seven yeas to eighteen nays — Randolph, Hayne, and Macon among the nays; about half the yeas Clay’s political opponents. Jacksonians treated the sequence as a violation of the democratic principle that the plurality man should have prevailed; Clay “took the lead in the House for Mr. Adams,” then tried by public speeches to reconcile the country, and was himself “crippled,” losing his place in the Democratic party and joining the National Republicans who became Whigs. Popular politics named the same story: House elects Adams; charge of a corrupt bargain. The charge followed Clay through the Adams years and into the defeat of 1828, when Jackson and Rush buried Adams and Clay together in the public mind, 178 electoral votes to 83.

Randolph duel, Panama, and the C&O

Panama mission politics — Adams’s effort to reverse the tide of the House election and inaugural doctrines of internal improvement — inflamed Senate and House and led to the duel between Clay and John Randolph of Roanoke. On Saturday 1 April 1826 Randolph came to Benton’s room at Brown’s Hotel, asked whether Benton was blood-kin to Mrs. Clay (Lucretia Hart), and declined Benton as second for that reason; he accepted Clay’s challenge with Col. Tatnall as second and Gen. Jesup for Clay, and told Benton secretly that he did not intend to fire at Clay — wanting a witness, not a second who knew. The provocation Benton traced to Randolph’s Panama speech daring the President and “the bravest of his compeers,” not necessarily a personal “scoundrel” charge. Cards were exchanged after the meeting; Benton ranked it among the last high-toned duels.

Under Adams, Clay as Secretary pressed the Panama mission (eventually sanctioned though it never took effect), urged force to protect Creek rights against Georgia surveyors when Adams preferred civil process, and watched the tariff fights culminating in 1828. The House’s million-dollar Chesapeake and Ohio Canal subscription — five annual installments, majority of forty-four — stood as a practical blessing of internal improvement. May 1828 Cabinet talk already feared an all-northern administration except Clay’s West. The American System, Panama, and canal together defined Clay’s State Department years as much as the bargain charge defined his enemies’ memory of them.

Compromise of 1833 — public settlement and secret history

In the nullification winter Clay’s compromise tariff swept the House — engrossment 105–71, final passage 119–85 — beside the Force Bill’s concurrent struggle. In the Senate Clay introduced the compromise on 12 February 1833 with a speech that the tariff was in imminent danger without change and that its fall would ruin manufacturing capital; the home-valuation amendment passed 26–16 with Clay, Calhoun, and Tyler among the yeas and Benton and Webster among the nays. Webster supported Jackson against nullification despite party opposition.

Secret history of the winter strips the pacificator legend. The Clay–Calhoun coalition of 1833 was a “hollow truce”: Calhoun humiliated by protective features yet kept alive to oppose Jackson and Van Buren. Floor quarrel later had Calhoun claiming he had Clay “on his back,” Clay answering he would not own him for the meanest of his slaves. Inside, via Letcher and Clayton: Josiah S. Johnson of Louisiana’s night visit to Letcher with word that Jackson would brook no delay and that decisive action against Calhoun (arrest for treason understood) was near; Letcher warned Calhoun in bed — “evidently disturbed.” Clayton of Delaware, Benton insisted, was the real architect; Letcher the conduit. Outside, Clay and Calhoun posed as peacemakers; inside, Clay saved protection and manufacturer support, Calhoun escaped Jackson’s coercion, and both freed their hands to reunite against Jackson and Van Buren. He refused to call the act “sacred and inviolable”; unlike the Missouri Compromise of 1820 (mutual consent, geographic line), the 1833 settlement was rivals laying the tariff aside for two presidential elections under patriotic alarm — received as deliverance, soon gone to neglect and oblivion.

Bank War, Whig leadership, and the Tyler rupture

Clay opened the Bank opposition of 1830–31 and stood for recharter against Jackson’s veto of 10 July 1832. The election of that year put Clay and Sergeant against Jackson and Van Buren with Bank and American System on the ballot; Clay took 49 of 288 electoral votes — “complete and overwhelming” defeat. In the panic session after deposit removal he joined Webster and Calhoun in the “great combination” against Jackson. Clay’s speech cast deposit removal as revolution hitherto bloodless, Caesar seizing the treasury, “premonitory symptoms of despotism,” Congress obliged to remedy or leave the nation “base, mean, and abject slaves.” Benton answered that Clay’s resolution was impeachment in substance and a Senate usurpation of the House’s power. Calhoun’s support renewed the mastery quarrel of 1833.

Harrison’s death and Tyler’s accession made the extra session of 1841 dominated by Clay. His programme: repeal of the Independent Treasury first; a national fiscal agent (bank) next — Tyler’s message also asking a bank. Clay personally carried sub-treasury repeal with instant effect and no substitute. The bankrupt act passed by logroll outside his original list. A first bank bill passed the Senate 26–25; Tyler’s qualified veto (1 August) and a second “fiscal corporation” followed by a second veto brought gallery hisses Benton defended as assaults on presidential dignity. Secret history of the rupture has Tyler and Whig dissidents plotting a third party, Tyler leading the second bill knowing he would veto — a “Jonas” to save the new combination. On 11 September the Cabinet resigned (Webster staying briefly); the Whig manifesto of that day declared twelve years’ fruits destroyed by Tyler. Clay remained Whig head; Tyler was later rejected by Democrats too. The quarrel, bank vetoes, and cabinet resignations opened the Tyler revenue and tariff struggles that preceded Texas.

Texas stance

Clay’s Texas weather was more surrounding politics than a single manifesto. The Florida treaty of 1819 had “given away Texas”; southern presidential candidates had accepted the cession to avoid slavery extension — context for later annexation fights. In the 1838 session the Texas republic applied for admission while still at war with Mexico; annexation meant annexing war. Preston of South Carolina’s resolution for a legislative expression toward a tripartite treaty was laid on the table 24–14 — the period not ripe. Clay–Calhoun debates of 1840–41 show the old allies concurred in nothing: Clay mocked Calhoun’s Edgefield letter; Calhoun answered that Clay had not led the tariff of 1816 (Lowndes had Ways and Means) and that Clay’s 1813 speech had opposed non-importation as too much protection. Tyler–Calhoun negotiations, the treaty’s Senate rejection, the election of 1844 as Polk’s platform and the defeat of Clay, and annexation by joint resolution as the mandate for expansion followed. Clay’s public identity in that canvass was the Whig alternative to immediate annexation’s war risk — defeated when the expansion mandate carried Polk and, under Tyler’s final push, Texas itself.

Compromise of 1850 — the Omnibus

Taylor’s only annual message recommended California’s admission if her constitution conformed, and territorial governments for New Mexico and Utah without mixing slavery — let them ripen to statehood and decide themselves. Clay answered with eight resolutions “to settle and close up once and for ever” all slavery contestation in one general compromise, and was placed at the head of a Committee of Thirteen to combine them into a single bill — the Omnibus. Benton opposed the whole plan: incongruous measures tacked together; California degraded by confounding her admission with general slavery agitation; futile; a concession to disunion threats; a repetition of the error of 1833. Settle each measure separately, beginning with California; leave consequences to God and the country; make no compromise with the threat of disunion. The Omnibus was not even a new draft: five old bills from different places on the calendar, tied together and required to be voted as one. Clay made ample report and elaborate speech; Benton answered in a counter-speech.

One Clay resolution declared slavery did not exist by law in the Mexican cession and that it was “inexpedient” for Congress to legislate for or against its introduction, territorial governments for Utah and New Mexico to be established without slavery restriction (California’s boundaries excepted). Jefferson Davis of Mississippi saw no compromise in a measure that gave the South nothing: never less than the Missouri line extended to the Pacific with specific recognition of the right to hold slaves south of that line, and slaves importable from any state before statehood. Clay met it with a declaration Benton called manly and lifelong: he utterly refused to plant slavery where it did not previously exist. Seward’s renewal of the Wilmot proviso failed, 23–33, Clay among the nays.

On 4 March Calhoun’s last speech — too weak to deliver, read by Mason — proclaimed Union or disunion, held Clay’s and Taylor’s plans inadequate, and demanded a constitutional amendment with a dual executive, one slave and one free — equivalent, Benton judged, to dissolution. Calhoun died 31 March; Butler’s eulogy compared him to Chatham. Benton’s own speech against the Omnibus ridiculed the “bill of thirty-nine sections — forty, save one — an ominous number,” denied the country’s “five gaping wounds” Clay counted on his fingers, and denounced giving Texas a veto over California’s admission as a “monstrosity.” He moved indefinite postponement so the bills might be taken one by one.

Taylor’s death on 9 July, after Fourth of July sun exposure, Benton called a public calamity: a Southern slaveholder of Union devotion and military reputation uniquely placed to settle the questions. Fillmore was sworn in the House chamber on the 10th — solemnity without joy — and soon made Webster Secretary of State and Crittenden Attorney General. The Omnibus was rejected: the Committee of Thirteen had reported for Clay’s plan; a committee nearly a quarter of the Senate should have insured concurrence; it did not. Incongruities too glaring for one yea or nay; injustice to California (a free-state applicant) too apparent; no spirit of concession in which giving and taking could make a compromise. Whatever was done would be ordinary majoritarian legislation. The Wilmot proviso was rejected again as unnecessary and irritating — not on non-intervention principle but because there had already been intervention. California’s separate admission followed, with Southern senators’ protest; Gwinn and Frémont took their seats. Clay’s last great design thus failed as Omnibus and succeeded only as the politics of separate bills under Fillmore — the pacificator’s method Benton had opposed since 1833, still naming the struggle.

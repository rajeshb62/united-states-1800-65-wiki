John Quincy Adams

John Quincy Adams (1767–1848) — son of John Adams, diplomat, Secretary of State, sixth President of the United States, and afterward Representative from Massachusetts — was the continuous diarist of American statecraft from the 1790s through the Mexican War. Day-by-day entries preserve missions, State, presidency, and House years; the same career sits inside the social and senatorial politics of the Union. The figure who emerges is not a party chieftain of the Jacksonian mold, but a servant of the Constitution who measured every deposit removal, petition gag, boundary note, and war bill against law, duty, and the rights of his constituents.

Early missions

Born 11 July 1767 and named for his great-grandfather John Quincy — colonial councillor and Indian negotiator then near death — he grew up in wartime education and European travel with his father before the formal diary begins. Few men left more abundant materials from cradle to grave. Washington sent him to Holland; small paper books precede the continuous journal, which opens 1 March 1795 at The Hague under a Greek motto and Bacon on Truth — neutrality problems, Batavian politics, self-training in languages and the law of nations, reading and self-examination when public pressure allowed. After Jay’s treaty, a London assignment to exchange ratifications consumed twenty-eight days of vexatious travel and arrived too late for that labor (Deas had already acted), but other pending questions kept him in negotiation; appointment to Portugal shifted to Prussia after Washington, days before leaving office, urged John Adams not to withhold promotion because the minister was the President’s son — “the most valuable public character we have abroad,” sure to prove “the ablest of our diplomatic corps.” Marriage to Louisa Catherine Johnson (1797) and the Berlin mission occupied the turn of the century. Recalled after the political revolution at home, he resumed the law under the disadvantage of long absence, lost a bankruptcy commissionership when Jefferson’s statute transferred appointments (Abigail reading ill will; Jefferson later disavowing knowledge), then served in the Massachusetts Senate and the United States Senate. The Senate chapter is the densest early political narrative: Jeffersonian measures, embargo and non-importation, party pressure, an independent course that alienated Federalist friends, committee minutes on commercial retaliation. In 1809 a major mission abroad — totally unsolicited — was confirmed by every Senate vote except personal enemies and two who opposed the mission rather than the man. He prayed for clearness of intellectual vision, courage, fortitude, and temperance; Harvard seniors and juniors asked publication of his lectures; on the twelfth wedding anniversary instructions arrived. Russian service, the Ghent negotiation, and the London mission fill the middle years before the homecoming of August 1817 — Neversink highlands at daylight, Tammany Hall dinner with De Witt Clinton, mixed gratitude and anxious forecast of the new scene.

Ghent, State, Florida, Monroe Doctrine

Adams later marked the anniversary of his arrival at Ghent as bringing placid recollections: the transactions there were the most fortunate occurrences of his life. Landing at New York 6 August 1817 after eight years abroad, he found Monroe returned from a long tour and the President’s House again habitable after the British burning. As Secretary of State he became the steady hand of Florida and the Adams–Onís settlement. Early cabinets take up Amelia Island and Galveston marauders, Buenos Ayres recognition postponed, Bagot, Hyde de Neuville, and Onís on the diplomatic calendar. Cabinet fights over Jackson’s seizure of Spanish posts found Crawford urging immediate disavowal and restoration lest the Administration own an unconstitutional war; Adams answered that to disavow and publish now would look like putting Jackson down, that Pensacola might be restored and its capture still justified, that he did not believe war would follow. He marked on the map the Trinity–Red River–mountain boundary authorized at the President’s, told Onís that Jackson had taken Pensacola without orders yet that no blame could be admitted against the General, and on 22 February 1819 signed and sealed the treaty copies with Onís — English and Spanish collated, Senate receiving the message with universal satisfaction. The same years bring South American recognition debates, fisheries consultations with Massachusetts members, and Missouri slavery agitation in which Adams rejected House restriction on the new State as incompatible with the Constitution and the Louisiana treaty, even while scorning slave-state bluster about oceans of blood and recording Rufus King as the great Northern champion against charges of personal ambition.

The climax of the second State term is the non-colonization / non-interposition policy of 1823. Adams drafts Rush instructions on neutral and belligerent rights; Cabinet debate on Canning’s proposal for a joint Anglo-American declaration runs for weeks. Adams opposes a pledge that would bind the United States against annexation of Texas or Cuba while yielding little; Southard inclines the same way; Monroe is averse to appearing subordinate to Britain. Calhoun stimulates panic after Cadiz falls to the French; Adams presses a dual rule — no American meddling in European politics, European abstention from subjugating any part of the American hemisphere. The final Monroe message, though varied from Adams’s draft, was conformable to his views. The same years saw growth of the Monroe Doctrine and the breaking of the Republican party that would send the succession to the House.

Presidency: Georgia, Panama, 1828

Succession politics fill 1824: rumor of a Crawford–Adams caucus coalition rejected; R. M. Johnson relating Calhoun’s proposed arrangement (Adams President, Jackson Vice-President, Clay at State) “not as a bargain”; Clay’s friends sounding Kentucky if the election went to the House — “Incedo super ignes.” Of the electoral votes Jackson led with ninety-nine, Adams eighty-four, Crawford forty-one, Clay thirty-seven. On 9 February 1825 the House, voting by States, gave Adams thirteen, Jackson seven, Crawford four — completed unexpectedly on a single ballot. Benton and the Jackson men forever branded a “corrupt bargain” when Clay took State. The presidency was beset by Georgia–Creek conflict, the Panama mission and Congress’s fight over mission funds, tariff and canal politics, Scott–Macomb command claims, Northeastern boundary agents, and the long campaign that ended in Jackson’s victory of 1828. In August 1828 he recorded a Jackson-party meeting haranguing three hours on the Administration’s unpardonable sins while he dined nearby. He left office without bitterness toward the forms of the Constitution, began 1829 in gloom — shaded lamp self-extinguished, first Psalm after prayer — crowded last New Year’s drawing-room, and on 11 February heard Jackson and Calhoun declared elected.

Two years of leisure at Quincy followed — Cicero completed over ten months, horticulture, Anti-Masonic conversation with Stone of the Commercial Advertiser on Letters upon Masonry, Eaton–Campbell–Ely intelligence treated as incomplete and not fully authentic. Richardson and Lincoln brought the Plymouth nomination; Turner of Scituate was put up to divide the vote. November 1830 returns: 1817 for Adams of 2565, with Thompson and Baylies trailing; he noted that no prior ex-President had sat in either House, that refusal would have been arrogance and ridicule, and that the call ended hope of quiet old age — “launched again upon the faithless wave of politics.” The Adams years and the Jackson triumph remade the social-political map of the Union.

House years: Bank, gag, petitions

From the Twenty-Second Congress his floor record becomes denser. Adams is repeatedly chairman of Manufactures; tariff paragraphs, Bank questions, nullification and the Force Bill, and Clay’s compromise fill the first sessions. In autumn 1833 he notes Jackson’s order removing the deposits, Duane’s dismissal, and Taney at the Treasury, telling intimates he will wait on the evidence before declaring for or against the Bank. Through 1834 he watches Senate censure, the presidential protest, and House struggle over custody of the public moneys. French spoliation under the treaty of 1831 and the Northeastern boundary recur; at the midnight close of the short session his resolution on the French dispute carries unanimously — two hundred votes and gallery applause, the first of his great House triumphs.

From 1835 the right of petition absorbs him. Pinckney’s select-committee report on abolition petitions drew extreme violence on the floor; Waddy Thompson would commit it to the flames or the hangman. Successive gag resolutions culminate, in the Twenty-Fifth Congress, in Patton’s rule that slavery petitions shall not be read or acted upon — which Adams answered, when his name was called, as a violation of the Constitution, of his constituents’ right of petition, and of his own freedom of speech. The special session of 1837 brings Sub-Treasury, specie suspensions, and renewed assaults on the call of the States for petitions. Atherton’s anti-petition resolutions continue the fight through the late 1830s. Benton, from the Senate, fights Bank and Independent Treasury in parallel winters; Adams holds the House petition memory of the same Union.

Amistad and Smithsonian

The Amistad densifies the record: Spanish ship into New London with African captives and Cuban claimants; murder and piracy charges; knotty questions for Judge Thompson and the Supreme Court — Adams follows as law and conscience. The Smithsonian bequest arrives when Rush recovers about half a million dollars; college interests lobby against a national university destructive of existing colleges; he recorded the Act of Congress and his own views of the trust, later briefing a new Secretary who receives the history with courteous indifference and helping defeat what he calls a “swindling Smithsonian bill” at a midnight close. Petition warfare never drops while these trusts and cases run.

Ashburton, Creole, Texas

Under Tyler, Adams on Foreign Affairs noted Lord Ashburton’s arrival and the Webster negotiation on the Northeastern boundary and related questions. The Creole — brig from Norfolk to New Orleans with tobacco and some 135 slaves; mutiny 7 November; course forced to a British port — filled the record with official narratives and the diplomatic storm. Texas annexation agitation rose through the early 1840s; Adams already saw the impulse of national aggrandizement “doomed beyond the reach of all but Almighty power.” Clay’s and Van Buren’s letters against annexation at this time appear; Jackson’s letter to Aaron V. Brown alleging treacherous surrender of Texas to Spain in 1819 becomes the text of Adams’s two-hour-plus Tremont Temple address of 7 October 1844. Oregon jurisdiction bills and China mission talk interleave with gag fights and censure attempts from Southern members.

Mexican War and death in the House

The Twenty-Eighth Congress closes in convulsive midnight business: Adams helps defeat a “swindling Smithsonian bill”; Tyler vetoes a steamer-payment prohibition and is overridden in both Houses — the first law passed over the veto “precisely as midnight came.” Polk’s rainy inaugural of 4 March 1845 opens the Twenty-Ninth Congress; Adams declines the Senate committee’s invitation to the ex-Presidents’ place, watches Polk and Tyler ride from Coleman’s, Dallas sworn, Polk’s half-hour address delivered in hard rain “to a large assemblage of umbrellas,” Taney administering the oath. He likens the day after a dying Congress to sudden calm after typhoon or hurricane. Texas annexed, Oregon contested, Mexican War debates fill the floor; he is renominated for the Thirtieth Congress even as war measures multiply. After a break in the continuous diary, a posthumous memoir in Adams’s hand dates his “decease” for useful purposes from the Boston seizure of 20 November 1846. The final return to Washington, last letter threads into early 1848, and death in the Capitol, 21–23 February 1848 — the old man stricken at his House post — close the life. Across three decades the same character holds: a New England constitutionalist who treated the House as a court of the people, measured executive power by the compact of the States, and made the right of petition the test of whether the Union still heard the governed.

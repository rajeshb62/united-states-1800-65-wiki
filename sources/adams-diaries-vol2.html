Overview

This is Volume 2 of the Library of America John Quincy Adams: Diaries, spanning 1821–1848, the companion to LOA Vol. 1 (1779–1821) already on this wiki. It is not Charles Francis Adams’s Lippincott Memoirs and not an Adams Papers letterpress Diary volume. Waldstreicher’s selection presents continuous reading texts of diary entries grouped in seven long chapters, with Chronology, Notes, and Index after the body.

Full Chapter-by-Chapter Breakdown

Chapter VIII — Secretary of State (1821–1825)

Opens on the interregnum day before Monroe’s second inauguration: official oath postponed, “no person qualified to Act as President.” Church at the Treasury building; visitors; evening writing. 5 March arrangements at the House hall; heads of departments accompany Monroe; reserved seats for diplomatic corps and members.

The chapter covers the second Monroe term at State: South American recognition and neutrality toward Spain’s colonies; cabinet discussions of Canning’s proposals and Holy Alliance fears (Calhoun “stimulates the panic” after Cadiz falls; Monroe appears briefly to despair of South America); draughts of message minutes and Rush instructions; foreign ministers and domestic patronage. Mid-span entries show Adams pressing written answers to Baronial notes and finding Monroe unsettled. Closing stretch runs through the House election aftermath of 1824–25: Webster committee notification of Adams’s election; written answer modeled on Jefferson’s precedent; invitation to Crawford to remain at Treasury; Military Ball with Monroe, Lafayette, Calhoun. Conversations with G. Sullivan report Calhounite threats—that Clay as Secretary of State would organize opposition under Jackson’s name; a preferred Calhounite cabinet slate (Poinsett, Cheves, McLean, Southard). Adams treats the intimidation as forewarning of systematic opposition. 3 March 1825 closes the Eighteenth Congress and Monroe’s administration: sleepless nights, State Department pressure, wife’s alarming health, Clay in the evening, proof of the inaugural Address from the National Intelligencer.

Chapter IX — President (1825–1829)

4 March 1825: Mrs. Adams desperately ill through the night; Adams’s prayer for country and name; escorted to the Capitol with Southard and Wirt; Monroe follows; Calhoun already sworn as Vice-President; inaugural from the Speaker’s chair; oath administered by Chief Justice Marshall from a volume of the Laws.

Administration years in this selection include foreign settlements (e.g. joy at Gallatin’s convention with Huskisson and Addington ending the Ghent slave-carrying controversy by a British payment over $1.2 million), cabinet business, internal improvements and Indian questions, and the rising Jackson opposition. Clay discusses the next presidential contest. Closing weeks of the term: Jackson avoids personal calls; newspaper explanations (publications against Mrs. Jackson; desire to avoid Clay) are recorded and denied as to Adams’s privity; Meridian Hill removal; Giusta retained by Jackson; inventory delays. 3 March 1829: last messages and bill signings; joint committee announces adjournment; Adams leaves the President’s House for Meridian Hill; accepts resignations of Rush, Porter, Southard, and Wirt—close of the Twentieth Congress and of his presidential public life in this framing.

Chapter X — Retirement (1829–1831)

4 March 1829: Andrew Jackson inaugurated. The leisure years at Quincy and occasional Washington passages: classical and literary study, family economy, correspondence, Anti-Masonic and local political soundings, and the path back toward elective office. The selection bridges defeat and the Plymouth-district return to the House (treated more fully as election machinery in the CFA Memoirs House volumes).

Chapter XI — Twenty-Second and Twenty-Third Congresses (1831–1835)

House service resumes. Committee labor, petitions, tariff and Bank politics, and personal parliamentary combat fill the span. Near the chapter end (30 January 1835), Adams attends the funeral of Warren Ransom Davis of South Carolina—Preston says Adams has lost a devoted friend; Davis’s near-last House words had asked God’s forgiveness for opposing Adams in 1828. Descending the eastern steps of the Capitol, Adams hears a pistol snap “like a squib”: the insane Lawrence’s attempt on President Jackson; both pistols miss fire; the funeral proceeds; sparse return to the House; adjournment on Adams’s motion.

Chapter XII — Twenty-Fourth and Twenty-Fifth Congresses (1835–1838)

Opens in foul winter weather with broken diary threads repaired as far as possible. Adams compares his public position to 1807–08: forced unanimous support for a resolution as counterpart to forced town-meeting politics after the Chesapeake, then Federalist vengeance; now he expects struggle without living to see reparation. French indemnity and Jackson’s possible rashness, presidential succession, and slavery-petition warfare dominate. Mid-chapter literary digressions (Gray, Plato) alternate with committee and library hours.

A set-piece of parliamentary defiance: on a call of yeas and nays Adams refuses to answer, declaring the proceedings unconstitutional; Speaker and two-thirds cry “Order!”; he completes “A direct violation of the Constitution of the United States” and sits; Waddy Thompson’s silvery sarcasm; laughter; vote 185–6 with Wise also refusing. Select committee on the duelling bill; presidential message on Pennsylvania’s call for aid against the Harrisburg mob that blocked legislative organization—Adams reads the refusal of federal aid as ominous precedent, fraud and violence triumphant, and a template by which servile insurrection might later meet the same non-assistance.

Chapter XIII — Twenty-Sixth and Twenty-Seventh Congresses (1839–1843)

Accepts, with extreme repugnance, the New-York Historical Society invitation to address the fiftieth anniversary of Washington’s inauguration—age, reputation, and decaying faculties warn him off; obligation prevails. Fragment delivery plans for a large church at noon. Political gossip (Clay, place-hunting) continues.

Amistad-related legal threads appear (discharge of Africans; Judge Thompson). Patronage seekers meet the limits of Adams’s influence (“Porter at the gate of Heaven”). Church-going and slavery’s absence from conventional sermons are noted. February 1843: Leavitt and Giddings discuss underhand movements for Texas annexation and a possible free-state address; Adams will concur but distrusts firmness of free-state members. Foreign Affairs committee on Cushing’s Ashburton report—boundary parts acceptable; slave-trade compromise sections full of “bad temper and absurd reasoning”; Adams warns Cushing of a coming global war of freedom against slavery headed by Britain, and conjures him not to commit to slavery’s side. Great Latimer petition of Massachusetts blankets his table; fifty-three petitions presented including dissolution-of-the-Union prayers; resolutions on slave-holding disproportion on the Supreme Court bench are blocked when the House flees into Committee of the Whole.

Chapter XIV — Twenty-Eighth, Twenty-Ninth, and Thirtieth Congresses (1843–1848)

Opens with reading of Prigg v. Pennsylvania—seven judges, every one dissenting from the others’ reasoning, all concluding the “transcendent omnipotence of Slavery” under the fugitive clause. Illness, lost days, and self-reproach at idleness recur (“I’ve lost a day”).

Texas annexation fever, Polk’s rise, and nativist–Irish Catholic riots in Philadelphia enter the political field of vision. Campaign and House work parallel the denser CFA Vol. XII narrative for 1844–46. Late entries follow the 20 November 1846 Boston seizure and slow convalescence (amanuensis-marked passages); return via Philadelphia and Baltimore to F Street on Louisa’s birthday 12 February 1847; autumn 1847 travel that feels like the “last great journey”; December 1847 scripture and lighthouse patronage at the Treasury with Secretary Walker—“every word henceforth… last words and dying speech.” Final dated verse lines 20 February 1848 (“Fair Lady, thou of human life…”) stand at the edge of the fatal House seizure the next day. Chronology, Notes, and Index follow the body in the LOA apparatus.

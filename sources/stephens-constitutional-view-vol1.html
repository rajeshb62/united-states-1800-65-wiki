Overview

Alexander H. Stephens, former U.S. congressman from Georgia and vice-president of the Confederate States, published this work after the war as a constitutional argument, not a campaign narrative. Volume I is complete in itself on the nature of the Union: whether the general government is strictly federal (a compact of sovereign states) or thoroughly national (one people territorially). Stephens holds the first; the war, he writes, was the collision of federation and centralism. “Slavery, so called,” was the question on which those organic principles finally met in arms, not the originating cause.

The form is colloquy at Liberty Hall, Crawfordville. Stephens frames the book as elaboration of conversations in spring and early summer 1867 with Northern visitors. Three composite interlocutors run through: Judge Bynum (Massachusetts, Radical Republican), Professor Norton (Connecticut, Conservative Republican), Major Heister (Pennsylvania, War Democrat). Real names are withheld; Stephens says the living prototypes will recognize the talks.

The title page states two volumes. Volume I covers the first head of the design (nature of the Union and the conflict of principles). Volume II (conduct of the war and results on free institutions) is in the companion source page. Body after title, copyright, dedication, list of illustrations, and contents: Introduction + Colloquies I–XII + Appendices A–F + Index. Page markers ----- PAGE N ----- separate leaves; OCR noise is typical (l’ro, pirnciples, TIE, 1IE, BROTHER2).

For this wiki the book is the principal Confederate constitutional statement after Appomattox from a Unionist-turned-secessionist who had made the November 1860 Milledgeville speech against immediate secession and then went with Georgia. It pairs with Fremantle (1863 Confederate tour) and the Nicolay–Hay / Grant military spines as the legal-political counterpart.

High-value passages

  • Dedication (16 Dec. 1867) — to “true friends of the Union under the Constitution” and of federative government as a barrier to “universal Monarchical Rule.”
  • Introduction — rejects the “irrepressible conflict” over African slavery as superficial; Missouri restriction as centralist manoeuvre (Jefferson’s “fire bell”); Cass/Douglas/Clay/Webster/Fillmore in 1850 and Northern anti-Lincoln voters in 1860 not a pro-slavery party.
  • Colloquy I — Northern surprise at Stephens’s secession; distinction of obedience to supreme law while it is law vs allegiance to the power that can unmake constitutions.
  • Colloquies II–IV — Story has “not a single fact to rest upon”; Marshall on the Confederation as a league of sovereigns; Convention not authorized to change the federal character; Vattel on perpetual confederacy without ceasing to be perfect states.
  • Colloquy VI close — even Hamilton, Morris, Wilson, King, Madison, Randolph sold the Constitution as federal in the state conventions.
  • Colloquies VII–IX — Webster–Calhoun; Calhoun “master of the arena”; Webster later a “Union of States” and “a bargain cannot be broken on one side and still bind the other.”
  • Colloquy X — Jackson’s farewell as compact evidence; no president from Jefferson to Lincoln elected who did not hold the compact view (Madison, Monroe, J. Q. Adams, Jackson, Van Buren, Harrison, Polk, Taylor, Pierce, Buchanan).
  • Colloquy XII — the secession right “of infinitely more importance to the Southern States than slavery, so-called, with its two thousand millions of capital invested in that institution.”
  • Appendix F — Holcombe 1858 on two races and “authority… and subordination” (Stephens’s documentary annex on the “minor question”).

Full breakdown

Front matter

Title: A Constitutional View of the Late War Between the States; its Causes, Character, Conduct and Results. Presented in a Series of Colloquies at Liberty Hall. Motto: “Times change and men often change with them, but principles never!” Two volumes; this file is Vol. I. Illustrations listed: Liberty Hall (frontispiece), Jefferson, Washington, Webster, Calhoun, Clay.

Dedication to friends of the Union under the Constitution without regard to party, and to friends of constitutional liberty and the federative system.

Introduction

Political convulsions, like fevers, come from violation of organic laws. Writers who make the war the result of two principles on African slavery are “superficial observers.” Opposing principles existed, but they lay in the organic structure of the general government: federal vs national / federation vs centralism. Slavery was the question that brought those principles onto the field. Jefferson, an emancipationist more ardent than many, opposed Missouri restriction as a centralist use of a subject that did not belong in federal councils. Cass, Douglas, Clay, Webster, Fillmore in 1850, and the million and a half Northern votes against Lincoln in 1860, are not a “Pro-Slavery Party.” The book’s object is those organic principles, now that “this element of discord is removed,” looking to preservation of constitutional liberty.

Design: (1) nature of the Union, causes, character of the war; (2) conduct of the war and results on free institutions. Materials too massive for one volume; Vol. I is complete on the first head. Method: colloquy, with Plato and Cicero as remote precedent, but really because 1867 talks at Liberty Hall began this way. Only fiction: names of the three Northern types (Bynum, Norton, Heister).

Colloquy I — Union speech of 1860; citizenship and allegiance

Bynum opens: the North was surprised Stephens went with secession after the 14 November 1860 Milledgeville speech, “one of the best Union speeches ever made.” Stephens will answer fully only after two preliminaries: citizenship pertains to the states; obedience is due to the supreme law while it is law, but allegiance is due to the paramount authority that can make and unmake constitutions. The question where that authority resides is the rest of the volume.

Colloquy II — Declaration, Confederation, Story

Inquiry: does paramount authority reside in the people of the United States as one nation, or in the people of the states severally? Historical sketch: independence declared by states; first union a compact of free sovereign communities. Joseph Story’s nationalist account “has not a single fact to rest upon.” Marshall from the Supreme Court bench: the Articles were a league between sovereign powers. Curtis: parties were free sovereign political communities.

Colloquy III — Articles and the Federal Convention

Thirteen states as distinct sovereigns entered the Confederation (1777/1781). Analysis of defects; the Convention was called to revise the Articles, not to change the basis of the Union—shown from the call and the states’ responses. No intention to change the federal character.

Norton: the new Constitution made a “thorough and radical change” that overthrows Stephens’s theory. Adjournment to the Liberty Hall walk; Heister will neither secede nor be seceded from.

Colloquy IV — Sovereignty unmoved; confederated republic

Ultimate sovereignty remains where it was under the Confederation. Story on the Convention’s first resolution answered. Government of states and for states; preamble does not merge sovereignties. Washington: confederated republic. Montesquieu: small republics uniting; Vattel: several sovereign states may form a perpetual confederacy without ceasing to be perfect states, putting “some restraint on the exercise” of sovereignty by “voluntary engagements.” Advocates admitted a federal republic, not national consolidation, whatever fears of abuse.

Colloquy V — The Constitution analysed

Face of the instrument: provisions, covenants, delegations of power as in the Articles. Antecedent facts (separate sovereignty, old law, evils, remedies, limited purpose of the Convention) kept in view. Declaration principles: governments instituted to preserve the several states. Next step is the states’ own action on the draft.

Colloquy VI — State conventions

Ratification debates: friends and opponents alike treated it as a federal constitution. Even Hamilton, Morris, Wilson, King, Madison, and Randolph, who had favored a national government in Convention, advocated it in the states as federal. Leading idea: Montesquieu’s confederated republic—an artificial state for foreign and interstate purposes, dissolvable while the small republics survive. Close: is the Constitution, from history and face, a compact between sovereign states?

Colloquy VII — Webster

Norton takes up Webster. Stephens works through Webster’s constitutional speeches as the nationalist case, setting up Calhoun.

Colloquy VIII — Calhoun

Calhoun on analysis vs “metaphysics” (Niles’s Register). The 1833 reply: Webster made “a few remarks only,” never a set rejoinder; “Mr. Calhoun stood master of the arena.” Stephens holds Webster later modified the 1830 compact language—Court speech 1839, Capon Springs 1851.

Colloquy IX — Webster later; Senate; Davis

Webster before the Supreme Court; letter to Baring Brothers; Capon Springs: Union of states, compact, bargain not binding if broken on one side. Court on state sovereignty; international comity; union of England and Scotland distinguished. Senate exposition 1838 (Calhoun’s 1833 first resolution sustained by about three-fourths of the states) and 1860 (nearly two to one). Jefferson Davis. Return: federal government, Washington’s confederated republic.

Colloquy X — Nullification to Jackson’s farewell

Nullification; Jackson on the Union; Jefferson; Kentucky Resolutions 1798. Senate: Wilkins, Calhoun, Grundy, Bibb, Clay. Compromise of 1833 on the protective policy; prosperity under those principles. No president from Jefferson to Lincoln elected who did not hold the government a compact of sovereign states (list includes J. Q. Adams). Supreme Court not the umpire between states and general government (Madison, Bibb, Marshall, Livingston). Jackson’s proclamation explained; farewell: maintain unimpaired the sovereignty of the states. Heister still does not see that secession “in disregard of her obligations” follows; Stephens: one thing at a time. Silence taken as compact truth established.

Colloquy XI — Right to withdraw

Each state judges infractions and the mode and measure of redress. Right to withdraw upon breach springs from the nature of the government. Compact broken by thirteen states. Authorities on the right to withdraw or secede: Webster, Story, Tucker, Rawle, de Tocqueville, Wade, Greeley, Lincoln. Course in 1860–61 left to posterity if the record is put up rightly; organic principles were the latent causes; next, the immediate question that brought them into physical conflict.

Colloquy XII — Strength of confederacy; slavery secondary

Is a confederated government too weak? On the contrary, strongest of all—J. Q. Adams and Jefferson. In secession was involved the right at the foundation of the federative system, of infinitely more importance to the Southern States than slavery, so-called, with its two thousand millions of capital. States may survive the Union; the Union cannot survive destruction of the states (index).

Overview

This is Volume VIII of Charles Francis Adams’s multi-volume Memoirs of John Quincy Adams, Comprising Portions of His Diary from 1795 to 1848, not the Library of America Diaries series and not an Adams Papers scholarly Diary volume. The title page of the extract reads Memoirs of John Quincy Adams… Edited by Charles Francis Adams. Vol. VIII. Philadelphia, J. B. Lippincott & Co., 1876 (copyright page 1875). Chapter numbering continues across the series: Volume VII closed Chapter XIV in late May 1828; this book resumes the same chapter without renumbering.

Volume VIII contains four body chapters:

  • Chapter XIV (Continued) — The Presidency (20 May 1828 through the close of the Administration; TOC p. 3)
  • Chapter XV — The Last Two Years of Leisure (4 March 1829; TOC p. 105)
  • Chapter XVI — The Twenty-Second Congress (4 March 1831; TOC p. 335)
  • Chapter XVII — The Twenty-Third Congress (4 March 1833; TOC p. 535)

Running page headers preserve month and year (e.g. [May, / 1828.] through 1833.]). OCR is Hathi-style page files (----- PAGE N -----); soft hyphenation and occasional garbled words appear. Front matter includes the full table of contents for the four chapters.

This CFA Memoirs volume covers the end of Adams’s presidency and his return to public life in the House of Representatives: the last months of cabinet business and the 1828 succession; Jackson and Calhoun declared elected 11 February 1829; the two years of leisure at Quincy with classical reading, private controversy, Anti-Masonic conversation, and the Plymouth district nomination; election to the Twenty-Second Congress (6 November 1830 returns: 1817 for Adams of 2565 votes); House committee work on manufactures; debate over the Bank of the United States; Jackson’s proclamation against South Carolina nullification; the Clay compromise tariff and the Revenue Collection (Force) bill; and the opening of the Twenty-Third Congress with Harvard’s planned LL.D. for Jackson, which Adams refuses to attend. Full Chapter-by-Chapter Breakdown

Chapter XIV (Continued) — The Presidency

Running-header years sampled in OCR: 1828–1829 (body open May 1828; first 1829 ~L3808). Body: lines ~98–4464 (20 May 1828 through eve of 4 March 1829). TOC places this chapter at p. 3.

Opening — May–summer 1828: privateers, treaties, cabinet threads

May 20th, 1828.—Mr. Derrick, of the Department of State, brought a letter from N. Williams, the District Attorney of Maryland, concerning the privateer Tuncal of Baltimore and related Bolivar (New York) privateer business; Mr. Brent brought copies of the proclamation of the treaty with the Eel River or Thornton party of Miamis. Cabinet and visitor threads continue from Vol. VII: Porter and Colombia, Barbour for Great Britain, Clay answers, army brevet and Scott–Macomb command questions, blank signatures left with Brent for proclamations and commissions during absences. August travel to Baltimore records a Jackson-party popular meeting in the square by Barnum’s House while Adams dines and receives visitors; McMahon harangues three hours on the unpardonable sins of the Administration.

Autumn–winter 1828–29: Scott claims, tariff abroad, close of the term

December business includes Northeastern Boundary agents (Gallatin and Preble) and compensation; Barbour from London reports the American tariff as unpopular in England as in South Carolina and expects Huskisson retaliation. Adams finishes a long manuscript on General Scott’s claim of command over Macomb and Gaines—perilous matter, written under a sense of duty, too long for a third rewriting. Scott arrives in a quasi-arrest posture; Porter drafts answers to the Military Committee.

January 1st, 1829.—The year begins in gloom. My wife had a sleepless and painful night. The dawn was overcast, and, as I began to write, my shaded lamp went out, self-extinguished… I began the year with prayer, and then, turning to my Bible, read the first Psalm. The last New Year’s drawing-room is crowded beyond former example. Clay and Porter business continues: Captain Turner of the Erie cutting out the Buenos Ayrean privateer Federal from St. Bartholomew (to be disavowed but with complaint against harbor authorities); Webster’s reported adhesion to the new Administration; Scott memorial answers. Adams begins reply materials to the Boston confederates’ pamphlet and confides papers to Jonathan Elliot for republication.

February–March 1829: electoral count; succession

February 11th, 1829.—This day the votes for the election of President and Vice-President of the United States for four years from the 4th of March next were opened, and Andrew Jackson, of Tennessee, declared President, and John C. Calhoun Vice-President. The President elect came into the city. Adams receives Baron Krudener with the Emperor Nicholas’s letter on the death of the Empress-mother, and hears delicacy over the near close of the Administration. Nominations are expected to be postponed; claims and Indian-agency accounts are left for the successor. Chapter XIV closes as the Presidency ends.

Chapter XV — The Last Two Years of Leisure

Running-header years: 1829–1831 (open 4 March 1829 ~L4465; first strong 1830 clusters mid-chapter; House election November 1830 ~L10425–10534). Body: lines ~4465–14357. TOC p. 105.

March 1829 onward: retirement, visitors, Eaton and Jackson court

March 4th, 1829 opens the leisure chapter. Diary entries mix Quincy and occasional Washington visitors, classical reading (Cicero completed over ten months), horticulture, and political intelligence. January 1830 conversations record the Eaton–Campbell–Ely controversy over Mrs. Eaton: Ely’s letter to Jackson, Cabinet confrontation, challenges and correspondence; Adams treats reports as incomplete and not fully authentic. McLean’s presidential ambitions, Calhoun as possible Nullification candidate, and Anti-Masonic prospects appear in talks with J. W. Taylor and others; Adams discounts McLean’s assurance that Jackson will not seek re-election.

Anti-Masonry and private study

Anti-Masonic conversation multiplies: Stone of the Commercial Advertiser proposes Letters upon Masonry and Anti-Masonry addressed to Adams for the abolition of Freemasonry; Adams presses that the work not favor continuance of the order and discusses Morgan’s manuscript, Clinton, and Knapp. William Wirt and Anti-Masonic candidacy threads appear later in related electoral talk. Adams continues classical and religious reading while receiving intelligence on tariff and nullification doctrine under Jefferson’s name, Georgia, and party realignment.

November 1830: nomination and election to the House

Richardson and Lincoln inform Adams that a district meeting has selected John Quincy Adams to represent Plymouth in the Twenty-Second Congress; the other party nominates Turner of Scituate to divide the vote and hope for Anti-Masonic support. Adams acquiesces: And so I am launched again upon the faithless wave of politics. National Republican convention handbills and Hedge’s letter confirm the nomination. November 6th newspaper returns: Twenty-two towns gave 2565 votes, of which 1817 were for John Quincy Adams, 373 for Arad Thompson (Jacksonite), 279 for William Baylies (federal), and 96 scattering… I am a member elect of the Twenty-Second Congress. Reflection follows: no prior ex-President has sat in either House; refusal would have been arrogance and ridicule; the call is a misfortune that ends hope of quiet old age. Chapter XV closes toward the March 1831 opening of the new Congress.

Chapter XVI — The Twenty-Second Congress

Running-header years: 1831–1833 (open 4 March 1831 ~L14358; Bank and manufactures density 1832; nullification winter 1832–33). Body: lines ~14358–22938. TOC p. 335.

Opening and committee work

March 4th, 1831 opens the congressional chapter. Adams takes his seat and is repeatedly named Chairman of the Committee of Manufactures. Diary records petitions, resolutions, tariff paragraphs, and committee struggles over reports. Anti-Masonic and National Republican alignments in New York and elsewhere continue as side conversation (Granger, electoral tickets, McLean angling).

Bank of the United States

Through the session the Bank of the United States appears in message distribution, Ways and Means silence or debate, Biddle-related talk, and investigative motions: new attack on the Bank; charges and indictments against the Bank; motions for investigating committees; Speight and Polk moves on bank portions of the President’s message; reference sometimes denied powers to send for persons and papers. Adams tracks yeas and nays and Western members’ shifts when public-land bribes are alleged to buy off internal-improvement votes.

South Carolina nullification, proclamation, tariff and Force bill

December 1832.—Major Hamilton, through J. W. Taylor, gives Adams the President’s proclamation to and against the South Carolina nullifiers. McDuffie jocularly reports that they have nullified the Tariff Act of last summer; Adams answers that ordinance and proclamation must fight it out. Adams offers a resolution calling for copies of the proclamation and the South Carolina ordinance; the House refuses consideration 106–64 (Jackson party, Unionists of South Carolina, and some National Republicans against; most National Republicans and nullifiers for). The President’s message against nullification, with proclamation, ordinance, etc., is later presented; referred to the Judiciary Committee under Bell of Tennessee; 25,000 copies ordered printed.

February 1833.—Clay’s Compromise tariff bill moves in Senate and House; Letcher moves recommitment with instructions to report Clay’s bill totidem verbis; engrossment carries 105–71 and final passage 119–85 as it swept like a hurricane. The Revenue Collection bill, otherwise called the Force bill, or, as the nullifiers call it, the Bloody bill, is taken up amid disorderly struggle. Adams prepares a minority report for the Committee of Manufactures after the majority directs discharge; Hoffman threatens to block reception and printing. Chapter XVI closes with the compromise session’s endgame.

Chapter XVII — The Twenty-Third Congress

Running-header year: 1833 (open 4 March 1833 ~L22939). Body: lines ~22939–23509; END OF VOLUME VIII. L23510. TOC p. 535.

March 4th, 1833 opens a short closing chapter. Electoral arithmetic of the late presidential contest is noted (Anti-Masonic and other tickets; Wirt’s seven electoral votes appear in related count talk). District nomination by ballot for the Twenty-Third Congress is recorded. At Quincy, Adams confers with President Quincy of Harvard on natural-history appointments (Fisher fund; Waterhouse vs. Nuttall and Harris). Quincy reports that President Jackson will visit Boston and that the Corporation will invite him, address him in Latin, and confer the degree of Doctor of Laws, with an invitation for Adams to attend. Adams refuses: personal relations with Jackson preclude friendly intercourse; as an affectionate child of Alma Mater he will not witness her disgrace in conferring highest literary honors upon a barbarian who could not write a sentence of grammar and hardly could spell his own name. Quincy answers with precedent (Monroe) and fear of party-spirit imputation; Adams is unsatisfied—time-serving and sycophancy of learned institutions—and stays home. Nursery and horticultural entries follow (walls, cherry-stones, strawberries, elm-seeds); distant cannonading of the President’s military review in Boston gives relish to solitary tranquillity. No period of life has ever yielded me so much quiet contentment as that which I enjoy with my family… cultivating… my seedling plants and trees… “Alteri Seculo” is the motto of all my plantations; but I am yet sensible and conscious that this life of pleasure is not a life of profit. END OF VOLUME VIII.

Overview

This is Volume IV of Charles Francis Adams’s multi-volume Memoirs of John Quincy Adams, Comprising Portions of His Diary from 1795 to 1848, not the Library of America Diaries series and not an Adams Papers scholarly Diary volume. Chapter XII — The Department of State—First Term

Running-header years sampled in OCR: 1817–1820. Month labels run August 1817 through February 1820. Body: lines ~78–22999 (~215,000 words; the entire volume after front matter).

NEW YORK, August 6th, 1817.—Immediately after daylight this morning the ship was within three miles of the highlands of Neversink, and the new Sandy Hook light-house in full sight, as well as the two old ones; all the lights were still burning. The morning was fine, and almost all the passengers soon came upon deck. The sun rose clear, and Venus was visible more than a quarter of an hour after she had risen; air and water both at 67. There were a number of vessels in sight, and among the rest a pilot-boat schooner, from which a pilot, named Bird, came on board at six. We had a fair and light breeze, which took us up to the wharf at New York, where we landed at one in the afternoon, immediately from the ship. The approach to New York was slow, and the termination of the voyage as agreeable as could be wished. The sentiments with which, after an absence of eight full and eventful years, I touched once more my native land were of a mingled nature: of the deepest gratitude to the Supreme Disposer for all the enjoyments and preservations of that long period, and particularly for the safe and happy close of the voyage just completed, together with an anxious forecast of the cares and perils of the new scene upon which I am about to enter… nothing but a firm reliance upon Him who has ever been my preserver, and the dispenser of every blessing, supported me from despondency. 7th. We had a continual succession of visitors… In the evening, Mr. Astor, Mr. Colden, and Mr. … came as a committee… and invited me to a public dinner… 11th. … I went with them to the public dinner at Tammany Hall. There were about one hundred persons present… among them Governor De Witt Clinton… 14th. Called on Mr. Trumbull, and found him with the frame for his large picture of the Declaration of Independence… left them transhipping to the sloop Margaret… for Washington.

In Washington he finds President Monroe returned from a long eastern and western tour; the President’s House is again habitable after the British burning of 1814. Monroe directs that Richard Rush is to succeed Adams at the Court of Great Britain and that instructions for Rush be drawn. Early Cabinet meetings take up Spain, South American insurgents, and the breaking up of marauding parties at Amelia Island and Galveston. Adams avows that it is not then expedient to acknowledge the Government of Buenos Ayres; the President postpones that determination. Bagot (British), Hyde de Neuville (French), and Don Luis de Onis (Spanish) fill the diplomatic calendar; fisheries proposals, etiquette with Senators, and the daily grind of the Department open the first term.

January 1st, 1818.—In the evening, and until eleven, I was writing remarks for Mr. Middleton’s draft of a report to the House of Representatives upon Amelia Island… 3d. … At the President’s, I mentioned to him again the proposal for the fisheries, to be made to Mr. Bagot… he directed me to ask Mr. Crowninshield, Mr. Holmes, Mr. Silsbee, Mr. Nelson, and all the members from Massachusetts representing districts interested in the fisheries, to call… for a consultation… Crowninshield had received dispatches with information of the surrender to us of Amelia Island on the 23d of last month. Mr. Calhoun afterwards came in with the same dispatches, and the President appointed a Cabinet meeting… to determine what should be done with Amelia Island.

Through 1818 the diary is dominated by the Florida–Seminole crisis and Jackson’s seizure of Spanish posts. In Cabinet, Crawford argues that if the Administration does not immediately declare itself and restore Pensacola, it will be held responsible for Jackson’s having taken it and for having commenced a war in violation of the Constitution. Adams answers that to disavow and publish now would look like a disposition entirely to put down Jackson; that Pensacola might be restored and its capture still justified; that he does not believe war would follow, though he admits it might. Hyde de Neuville is extremely anxious for the preservation of peace; Adams marks on the map the boundary authorized at the President’s—the Trinity from its mouth to its source, then a line north to the Red River… then crossing to the Rio del Norte… or the summit of a chain of mountains… there stop, or take a line west to the Pacific. De Neuville offers to carry the line to Onis at Bristol. Adams says Onis will be told that Jackson had taken Pensacola without orders and upon his own responsibility; that the place would be restored, but that no blame could be admitted as attaching to General Jackson, and strong charges would be made against the Governor of Pensacola and the Commandant at St. Mark’s. Arbuthnot and Ambrister executions, Bagot’s agitation over court-martial news, and Calhoun’s orders for the surrender of Pensacola interleave with Onis’s notes.

WASHINGTON, January 1st, 1819. Friday.—Began the year by commencing the draft of a dispatch to R. Rush, London, and then finished the fifth volume of this diary… Went with Mrs. Adams to pay the New Year’s visit at the President’s… The President’s house was also more crowded than I ever saw it on a similar occasion. Mr. Daschkoff, the Russian Minister, paid me likewise a visit this morning. It is the first time he has been at Washington since I have been in office… 2d. I finished my draft of a dispatch to R. Rush… to instruct Rush to inform Lord Castlereagh that the President intended before long to recognize the Government of Buenos Ayres as independent… When I read my draft to the President, he thought it best to consult the Cabinet… They were all startled at the paragraph announcing it as the President’s intention at no remote period to recognize the Government of Buenos Ayres… Mr. Pedersen, the Danish Minister, came… upon the project of a treaty of commerce between the United States and Denmark…

Onis delivers a projet of a treaty for the cession of the Floridas by Spain to the United States, for the settlement of the western boundary, and for the adjustment of claims of indemnities… in seventeen articles; Adams promises a counter-projet. After protracted negotiation, 22d (February 1819): Mr. Onis came at eleven, with Mr. Stoughton… The two copies of the treaty made out at his house were ready… We exchanged the original full powers on both sides… I read the English side, which he collated, and he the Spanish side, which I collated. We then signed and sealed both copies on both sides—I first on the English and he first on the Spanish side… Mr. Onis took with him his executed copy of the treaty, and I went over with ours to the President’s… Brent takes the message with the treaty to the Senate; Fromentin reports it was received with universal satisfaction. The same season brings Jackson to the city, Forsyth’s appointment as Minister to Spain, and Rufus King’s long political conversations (including the British West India trade article).

Later entries turn to the Missouri question. Congress had failed at the last session to make the Territory of Missouri a State, owing to a restriction introduced by the House excluding slavery from the State and declaring free all persons to be born there after a certain period; the Senate struck out the provision, and the bill failed by disagreement. Adams records the violent agitation among members from the slave-holding States, and writes: The slave-drivers, as usual, whenever this topic is brought up, bluster and bully, talk of the white slaves of the Eastern States, and the dissolution of the Union, and oceans of blood; and the Northern men, as usual, pocket all this hectoring… Crawford… relies on the support of the slave-drivers… On this particular question I did not approve of the attempted restriction upon the State of Missouri, because I believe it not compatible either with the Constitution of the United States or with the Louisiana Treaty. But I think Crawford’s toast not remarkable for delicacy towards the Legislature, while the question is pending before them…

January 1st, 1820.—I attended at the Treasury Office a meeting of the Commissioners of the Sinking Fund, which Mr. Crawford had appointed at two o’clock… The volume closes still deep in the Missouri agitation. 27th. Called upon Mr. R. King at his lodgings at Crawford’s Hotel, Georgetown. Found him still absorbed in the Missouri slave question, upon which he has been the great champion of freedom and the Northern interest. The partisans of slavery have spread abroad the idea that he has been actuated… by motives of personal ambition… King, however, indulges hopes that it may survive the present question. END OF VOL. IV.

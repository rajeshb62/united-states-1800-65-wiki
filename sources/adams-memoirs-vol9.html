Overview

This is Volume IX of Charles Francis Adams’s multi-volume Memoirs of John Quincy Adams, Comprising Portions of His Diary from 1795 to 1848, not the Library of America Diaries series and not an Adams Papers scholarly Diary volume. The title page of the extract reads Memoirs of John Quincy Adams… Edited by Charles Francis Adams. Vol. IX. Philadelphia, J. B. Lippincott & Co., 1876 (copyright page also 1876). Chapter numbering continues across the series: Volume VIII closed Chapter XVII in early summer 1833 at Quincy after Jackson’s Harvard LL.D.; this book resumes the same chapter without renumbering.

Volume IX contains three body chapters:

  • Chapter XVII (Continued) — The Twenty-Third Congress (from 24 June 1833; TOC p. 3)
  • Chapter XVIII — The Twenty-Fourth Congress (4 March 1835; TOC p. 214)
  • Chapter XIX — The Twenty-Fifth Congress (2 April 1837; TOC p. 344)

Running page headers preserve month and year (e.g. [June, / 1833.] through 1838.]). OCR is Hathi-style page files (----- PAGE N -----); soft hyphenation and occasional garbled words appear. Front matter includes the full table of contents for the three chapters.

This CFA Memoirs volume covers Adams’s House years under the later Jackson and early Van Buren administrations: the removal of the deposits and the Bank war; the Senate’s censure of Jackson and the presidential protest; French claims under the treaty of 4 July 1831; Northeastern and Ohio–Michigan boundary disputes; Anti-Masonic and district electoral politics; the flood of abolition petitions, the Pinckney select-committee report, and successive gag resolutions (including Patton’s), with Adams’s recorded protest that the gag violated the Constitution and the right of petition; Texas recognition and annexation agitation; the Lovejoy killing at Alton; the Cilley–Graves duel and House investigation; the Sub-Treasury / divorce of Bank and State; and Indian-hostility and Cherokee / New Echota debate. Full Chapter-by-Chapter Breakdown

Chapter XVII (Continued) — The Twenty-Third Congress

Running-header years sampled in OCR: 1833–1835 (body open 24 June 1833; first dense 1834 ~L1101 area; close early March 1835). Body: lines ~79–9172. TOC places this chapter at p. 3.

Opening — June–July 1833: Ghent anniversary; Jackson in New England

June 24th, 1833.—This day is one of those anniversaries which bring to my mind placid recollections. It is that of my arrival at Ghent… The transactions at Ghent were the most fortunate occurrences of my life. Diary continues with President Jackson’s New England tour: receptions, sickness under Dr. Warren at the Tremont House, postponed Bunker Hill and Harvard ceremonies, then the Cambridge LL.D. (Quincy address in English; senior oration in Latin), Everett’s address on Bunker’s Hill, and the ride to Salem. Dr. Waterhouse dines and reports captivation by Jackson’s manners yet extreme debility; Adams answers that four-fifths of the sickness is trickery and that a Doctorate for which an apology was necessary was a cheap honor and a sycophantic compliment. Jackson cuts short the tour and returns toward Washington via Concord and Roxbury.

Autumn 1833: removal of the deposits; Taney for Duane

Late September–October entries record President Jackson’s order to withdraw the deposits of public moneys from the Bank of the United States and place them in sundry State banks, a paper read to the Cabinet on the 18th of the month, the dismission of William J. Duane, and the appointment of Roger B. Taney as Secretary of the Treasury—upon all which Adams takes time for reflection. November conversations with Huntington (Connecticut) anticipate Stevenson’s re-election as Speaker and a bank committee that will report approving the removal; Biddle assures that the bank is prepared for the crisis. Adams tells E. Wyer he is at present neither for nor against the bank and will wait for the evidence submitted to Congress on removing the deposits. Richard Rush’s intrusion into the deposit controversy is treated as painful and ruinous to Anti-Masonry in Pennsylvania.

1834: Bank war on the floor; Senate censure and Jackson protest

Through the session the diary tracks deposit banks, committee investigations, Speakers and Ways and Means under Polk, and House–Senate conference fights over appropriations. April 1834.—The President of the United States this day sent to the Senate a protest against their resolution of censure upon him for his recent interferences with the public revenues, and he calls upon them to enter his protest upon their journals. Great excitement. Poindexter moved that it should not be received. Clay, Webster, and Preston are absent. In the House, Wise and Baylie Peyton offer competing resolutions on custody of the public moneys and on whether the Senate’s censure infringed the prerogatives of the House.

Boundary, Texas, France; close of the session March 1835

Resolutions and messages touch the Northeastern boundary negotiation with Britain, the province of Texas and the Mexican boundary under the treaty of 22 February 1819, and the French Chamber’s refusal to appropriate for the twenty-five millions of francs of the treaty of 4 July 1831. At the midnight close of the short session, Adams’s resolution on the dispute with France is carried unanimously—two hundred votes, followed by clapping of hands in the gallery (CFA editorial note: the first of the great triumphs of Mr. Adams in the House). The Fortification bill fails after the Senate disagrees to a three-million discretionary amendment and the conference report comes after midnight without a quorum; at twenty minutes past three A.M. the House adjourns without day after Bell’s thanks address.

Chapter XVIII — The Twenty-Fourth Congress

Running-header years: 1835–1837 (open 4 March 1835 ~L9173; slavery-petition density 1835–36; Texas and Pinckney May 1836; district returns November 1836; blank after early January 1837). Body: lines ~9173–14767. TOC p. 214.

Opening — March 1835: Speaker Bell; Ohio–Michigan; French news

March 4th, 1835.—Rose much exhausted… Adams retains no unkind feeling toward late Speaker John Bell after the vote of thanks, yet suspects Bell’s motive in making him chairman on the northern boundary of Ohio, Indiana, and Illinois—a service impossible honestly to perform without alienating three States. Conversation with Secretary of War Cass on the Ohio–Michigan disputed territory: Cass says the President shares Adams’s view and would dispossess Ohio by force if she took the lands; Michigan might compromise, Ohio not. Rumor circulates that the French Chamber of Deputies has rejected the appropriation for the 1831 treaty. Anti-Masonic and Whig nomination talk for Edward Everett as Governor appears in evening visits.

Petitions, slavery, Pinckney report

Jarvis of Maine, at Owens of Georgia’s request, offers a resolution that the House will not entertain any petitions for the abolition of slavery in the District of Columbia; Adams moves to lay on the table and is rejected 123–66; Wise substitutes declarations that Congress has no power to abolish slavery in the District. May 1836.—H. L. Pinckney presented the report of the select committee to whom all the abolition petitions were referred, and said that the report had the unanimous assent of the committee… It was immediately attacked with extreme violence… Waddy Thompson said that he would commit it to the flames or to the hangman. Hardin, a committee member, dissents. Related floor fights include Texas acknowledgment motions (Thompson would amend to ask the President’s opinion on recognizing Texan independence) and printing / recommitment of the Pinckney report with instructions that Congress has no constitutional authority to abolish slavery in the District.

District politics 1836; Michigan boundary; blank before Chapter XIX

November returns from the Twelfth Massachusetts district show a perilous passage: nomination by Anti-Masonic meeting; Whigs and Democrats make no opposing nomination but do not fully consolidate; Abington, Rochester, and Weymouth fragments appear in the newspapers while most Whig papers omit the district. Adams reflects that the strait will not be repeated. Extended entries denounce the congressional settlement of the Ohio–Michigan boundary as usurpation of judicial power and robbery of Michigan property, forced through by electoral arithmetic of the three Northwestern States—against which Adams alone spoke three hours and found no support. CFA records a blank in the diary extending beyond the remainder of the Twenty-Fourth Congress, regretting silence on the memorable attempt to censure Mr. Adams on the 23d of January [1837] after presentation of twenty-one petitions, some purporting to come from slaves—the first of his great struggles in that cause—and points readers to Letters from John Quincy Adams to his Constituents… (Boston: Isaac Knapp, 1837), with introductory remarks initialed by John G. Whittier.

Chapter XIX — The Twenty-Fifth Congress

Running-header years: 1837–1838 (open 2 April 1837 ~L14768; special session Sub-Treasury and petition fights; regular session gag, Texas, duel, Indian bill). Body: lines ~14768–23712; END OF VOLUME IX. L23713. TOC p. 344.

Opening — April 1837: slavery reading; Whitney libel; Taney on the bench

April 2d, 1837.—I wrote to Mr. Biddle, and read the printed letter of Gerrit Smith to the Rev. James Smylie… Smylie defends slavery as an institution sanctioned by the Scriptures… This subject of slavery, to my great sorrow and mortification, is absorbing all my faculties. Baltimore journey for the Whitney libel case against Leakin and Barnes; Circuit Court held by Roger B. Taney, now Chief Justice of the United States, and Upton Heath. Testimony is delayed; Adams returns to Washington.

Special session: Sub-Treasury, specie suspension, right of petition

Bell breaks out against the Sub-Treasury project of the message; Cambreleng supports it and challenges the opposition to propose their Bank of the United States. Adams offers resolutions calling for Mexican boundary and Texas-annexation correspondence and for Northeastern-boundary papers since Jackson’s 15 June 1836 message. He moves that the Clerk print State acts relating to the suspension of specie payments by banks. When Cushman moves to suspend the call of the States for petitions, Adams assails the resolution as an abridgment of the right of petition; Cushman withdraws after his own party calls for withdrawal. Anti-Masonic convention visitors (Denny, Middleswarth) appear at Brown’s Hotel.

Gag, Texas annexation, Lovejoy, Cilley–Graves

Patton offers a resolution that no petitions relating to slavery or the slave trade in any State, district, or Territory shall be read, printed, committed, or in any manner acted upon. Suspension of the rules carries 136–65; previous question and resolution carry. When my name was called, I answered, “I hold the resolution to be a violation of the Constitution, of the right of petition of my constituents, and of the people of the United States, and of my right to freedom of speech as a member of this House.” —amidst a perfect war-whoop of order. Adams moves that the answer be entered on the journal; after further struggle it is entered though amendment of the journal is laid on the table. Texas annexation memorials and remonstrances crowd the calendar; Adams argues for a select committee rather than Foreign Affairs alone.

November 1837 diary records the Alton, Illinois, mob that shot Lovejoy dead defending a fourth printing-press, broke the press, and threw it into the river: Such men are often fated to be martyrs; and he has fallen a martyr to the cause of human freedom. CFA editorial note inserts the Graves–Cilley duel (William J. Graves of Kentucky and Jonathan Cilley of Maine, the latter killed), which added to party and sectional excitement; Fairfield moves a committee of seven to investigate. Adams presents large batches of petitions under the gag, and occasional non-slavery petitions (women for Mexican arbitration; congress of nations) that escape to Foreign Relations.

Indian Hostility bill; Georgia and New Echota; close of the volume

Late May 1838 committee of the whole on the Indian Hostility bill: Holsey of Georgia delivers invective against Adams’s Administration and glorifies Georgia’s defiance of the Supreme Court decision on Indian lands, which Jackson prevented being executed—arguing that without Executive concurrence there is no law, and Georgia would laugh the Court to scorn. Naylor denounces the treaty so called of New Echota of December 1835 as nefarious villainy already exposed when the Senate advised and consented. Dawson of the Georgia “Indian party” justifies by Adams’s Plymouth oration and older States’ practice. Bell of Tennessee reviews Jackson-administration measures and his own defense against Jacksonite slander, then offers an amendment to apply two millions to coaxing Cherokees rather than driving them by bayonet. Horace Everett, Crary, and Turney follow amid a thin, drowsy House. Between nine and ten Adams reaches home. END OF VOLUME IX.

Overview

Volume Six covers mid–late 1862 into early 1863: Pope’s Virginia campaign and Second Bull Run; French intervention in Mexico; European diplomacy and the Alabama; Lincoln’s refusal of mediation; Hunter’s emancipation experiment and border-state appeals; the decision for and postponement then announcement of emancipation; Antietam; McClellan’s removal; Fredericksburg; Chase’s financial measures; the Seward–Chase resignation crisis; Perryville and Murfreesboro; West Virginia admission; churches; military governors; colonization; Missouri guerrillas; the final Emancipation Proclamation; Negro soldiers; and retaliation policy.

Chapter 14 — West Virginia Admitted

Wheeling after a recess, and on the 20th day of August, 1861, adopted another ordinance provid- ing for the formation of a new State, composed of thirty-nine counties, which was to be called the State of Kanawha. An election was ordered for the fourth Thursday of October to vote upon the proposition and elect delegates to a constitutional convention. The October vote stood 18,408 for the new State and only 781 against; the convention at Wheeling (26 Nov. 1861–18 Feb. 1862) framed a constitution of forty counties under the name West Virginia, ratified 18,862 to 514 on 3 Apr. 1862. The restored Virginia legislature at Wheeling assented 13 May; the Senate Territories committee reported admission with gradual emancipation beginning 4 July 1863; Senate yeas 23 nays 17 (14 July 1862), House 96–55 (10 Dec.). The chapter then turns to Cabinet consideration of the bill, Lincoln’s opinion, approval, the reassembled convention’s acceptance of Congress’s conditions, the proclamation admitting West Virginia, inauguration of the new state government, abolition of slavery in the State, and the Peirpoint government’s removal to Alexandria.

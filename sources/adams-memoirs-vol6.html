Overview

This is Volume VI of Charles Francis Adams’s multi-volume Memoirs of John Quincy Adams, Comprising Portions of His Diary from 1795 to 1848, not the Library of America Diaries series and not an Adams Papers scholarly Diary volume. The title page of the extract reads Memoirs of John Quincy Adams… Edited by Charles Francis Adams. Vol. VI. Philadelphia, J. B. Lippincott & Co., 1875 (copyright entry 1875). Half-title OCR includes VOL. VI. Chapter numbering continues across the series: Vol. V closed Chapter XIII in early 1822; this book resumes the same chapter and then opens Chapter XIV with the presidency.

Volume VI contains two body chapters:

  • Chapter XIII (Continued) — The Department of State—Second Term (2 June 1822 through the House election of 9 February 1825 and the close of Monroe’s administration; TOC p. 3)
  • Chapter XIV — The Presidency (4 March 1825 through early summer 1825; TOC p. 518)

Running page headers preserve month and year (e.g. [June, / 1822.]). OCR is Hathi-style page files (----- PAGE N -----); soft hyphenation and occasional garbled words appear. Front matter includes a short table of contents for Chapters XIII (Continued) and XIV. Charles Francis Adams’s editorial note at the head of Chapter XIV warns that presidential materials are not continuous—pressure of visitors and official duty left gaps and minutes rather than full narrative.

This CFA Memoirs volume covers the climax of Adams’s State Department service and the first months of his presidency: South American recognition and commercial missions; Stratford/George Canning, the Holy Alliance panic, and the non-colonization / non-interposition policy that becomes the Monroe message of 1823; the long presidential succession struggle among Adams, Jackson, Crawford, Clay, and Calhoun; the House election of 9 February 1825; Clay as Secretary of State; inauguration 4 March 1825; and early cabinet business (Rush, Barbour, Southard, Wirt; Porter enquiry; Georgia–Creek and Cuban questions). Prefer diary date + chapter when building event pages; cite this book as CFA Memoirs Vol. VI.

Full Chapter-by-Chapter Breakdown

Chapter XIII (Continued) — The Department of State—Second Term

Running-header years sampled in OCR: 1822–1825. Body: lines ~74–22308 (2 June 1822 through the close of Monroe’s administration and the transfer of power). TOC places this chapter at p. 3.

June 2d, 1822.—General D. Parker came in, and detained me at an interview of more than three hours. Parker was brought in as Chief Clerk of the War Department by Dr. Eustis… Both the President and the Secretary of War have reasons for disliking, and, as he evidently thinks, for dreading Parker… Crawford wrote him a note (6th May)… offering him a clerkship… which he declined… This avidity of Crawford to secure Parker in his interest is easily accounted for. He knows that great use may be made of him against both the President and Calhoun… The opening of the volume is thus already a map of presidential undercurrents—Armstrong’s attacks on Monroe, Scott as a Calhoun champion, Russell’s letter and the President’s search for papers, and Adams’s insistence that he must stand upon his own support. Cabinet meetings immediately take up Porto Rico privateers and West India piracy, Creek and Florida awards, and Canning’s visits on South American recognition and the slave-trade.

Through the summer and fall of 1822 the diary tracks recognition of the South American Governments, commercial missions without exclusive advantages, and European embarrassment at the American example. Adams tells Monroe that running a race with England for special privilege would be neither wise nor honest; mutual advantage and reciprocity are all that should be asked. Canning continues long interviews on slave-trade search, boundary, and colonial trade; Hyde de Neuville and French commercial questions, Russian agents, and agents of the new republics recur. Cabinet and congressional politics harden around Crawford’s “Radicals,” Calhoun’s presidential expectations, and newspaper warfare.

July–November 1823 bring the crisis that the editor later labels the first hint of the Monroe doctrine. Adams drafts instructions to R. Rush on maritime, belligerent, and neutral law and a project of Convention on neutral and belligerent rights. Cabinet debate on Canning’s proposals for a joint Anglo-American declaration against European recolonization of Spanish America runs for weeks. Adams opposes a joint declaration that would give Britain a pledge against American annexation of Texas or Cuba while yielding little in return; Southard inclines the same way; Monroe is averse to any course that appears subordinate to Britain and suggests a special Minister to protest Holy Alliance interposition. Calhoun stimulates panic after Cadiz surrenders to the French; Monroe for a time despairs of the South American cause. Adams prepares minutes for the President’s message, drafts on communications from Baron de Tuyl, and presses a firm non-interference dual rule: the United States will not meddle in European politics, and European powers must equally abstain from subjugating any part of the American hemisphere. The final paper, though varied from Adams’s original draft, is conformable to his views; paragraphs on Greece, Spain, Portugal, and South America are drawn in the spirit he had urged.

January 1st, 1823 opens with a New Year poem and Anglo-Russian slave-convention salaries; January 1st, 1824 opens on the Richmond anti-caucus explosion (Tyler’s report indefinitely postponed 77–76) and Webster’s Greek resolution, which Adams fears will compromise quiet work at Constantinople. Through 1824 the diary is dominated by succession politics. Rufus King reports rumor of a Crawford–Adams caucus coalition for President and Vice-President; Adams says such overtures were made and rejected. R. M. Johnson relates Calhoun’s proposed arrangement (Adams President, Jackson Vice-President, Clay Secretary of State, Johnson Treasury) “not as a bargain or coalition, but by the common understanding of our mutual friends.” Clay’s friends (Letcher and others) sound Adams on Kentucky and Western votes if the election goes to the House. Adams writes “Incedo super ignes.”

January 1st, 1825.—… Richard Forrest brought me, to authenticate, an Act of Congress granting to General La Fayette a sum of two hundred thousand dollars and a township of land… Robert P. Letcher… told me… the members of the Kentucky Legislature would… recommend… General Jackson… But I might rely upon it they would have no effect. The vote of Kentucky in the House was fixed and unalterable… 9th February 1825.—May the blessing of God rest upon the event of this day!—the second Wednesday in February, when the election of a President of the United States for the term of four years, from the 4th of March next, was consummated. Of the votes in the electoral colleges, there were ninety-nine for Andrew Jackson… eighty-four for John Quincy Adams… forty-one for William Harris Crawford… and thirty-seven for Henry Clay… in all, two hundred and sixty-one… the House of Representatives immediately proceeded to the vote by ballot from the three highest candidates, when John Quincy Adams received the votes of thirteen, Andrew Jackson of seven, and William H. Crawford of four States. The election was thus completed, very unexpectedly, by a single ballot.

The next day a House committee (Webster, Vance, Archer) formally notifies him of the choice; he returns a written answer on the Jefferson precedent. He invites Southard to remain at the Navy, offers Crawford the Treasury (declined in friendly terms), and determines to offer the Department of State to Clay—due to his talents and services, to the Western section of the Union, and to the confidence of the friends who had supported him. Calhoun’s circle, Johnson’s disclosures, and Benton/Poinsett moves are read as a system of opposition to bring in Jackson under Calhoun’s auspices. Nominations and leave-taking with Monroe fill the last days of the second-term chapter as Adams prepares to enter the presidency.

Chapter XIV — The Presidency

Running-header years sampled in OCR: 1825. Body: lines ~22309–23604 (4 March 1825 through mid-May 1825; END OF VOL. VI). TOC places this chapter at p. 518.

Charles Francis Adams opens with an editorial caveat: materials for this important period are not continuous; constant interruption of visitors and imperative official duties left gaps never afterwards filled; much of what remains is minutes for a narrative rather than the narrative itself, founded largely on the visits of persons drawn to the President.

March 4th, 1825.—After two successive sleepless nights, I entered upon this day with a supplication to Heaven, first, for my country; secondly, for myself and for those connected with my good name and fortunes, that the last results of its events may be auspicious and blessed. About half-past eleven o’clock I left my house with an escort of several companies of militia and a cavalcade of citizens, accompanied in my carriage by Samuel L. Southard, Secretary of the Navy, and William Wirt, Attorney-General, and followed by James Monroe, late President of the United States, in his own carriage. We proceeded to the Capitol, and to the Senate-chamber… John C. Calhoun presiding… having been previously sworn into office as Vice-President… I repaired to the hall of the House of Representatives, and, after delivering from the Speaker’s chair my inaugural address to a crowded auditory, I pronounced from a volume of the laws held up to me by John Marshall, Chief Justice of the United States, the oath faithfully to execute the office of President of the United States… After exchanging salutations with the late President… I returned to my house… a crowd of visitors… then… to the President’s house… and in the evening attended the ball…

Early presidency business is cabinet formation and foreign and military detail. Nominations include Henry Clay Secretary of State; Richard Rush of Pennsylvania Secretary of the Treasury; James Barbour of Virginia Secretary of War; Alexander Hill Everett to Spain; chargés to the Netherlands, Portugal, Buenos Ayres, Guatemala, and Brazil; Roger Jones Adjutant-General; and batches of customs, land-office, navy-agent, Florida, and consular renominations. Adams resists a principle of rotation at the expiration of commissions that would make government a perpetual scramble for office. Clay prepares instructions for Rufus King; conversations with General Brown turn on army rank (Gaines vs. Scott) and Mackinaw troops; Southard brings the Court of Enquiry on Captain Porter (Foxardo / Porto Rico). Barbour reports Mexican designs on Cuba and the critical state of Georgia and the Creek Indians, with Crowell’s letter complaining of the Governor of Georgia. Clay urges removal of Sterret, Naval Officer at New Orleans, as a clamorous reviler of the Administration after the election; Adams accepts the principle that after decision no officer at the President’s pleasure should hold open disparagement, but insists on specific proof and delicacy in application. The volume ends as Adams renews the attempt to journalize morning and evening: END OF VOL. VI.

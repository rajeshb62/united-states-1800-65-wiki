Overview

This is Volume VII of Charles Francis Adams’s multi-volume Memoirs of John Quincy Adams, Comprising Portions of His Diary from 1795 to 1848, not the Library of America Diaries series and not an Adams Papers scholarly Diary volume. The title page of the extract reads Memoirs of John Quincy Adams… Edited by Charles Francis Adams. Vol. VII. Philadelphia, J. B. Lippincott & Co., 1875. Chapter numbering continues across the series: Volume VI opened Chapter XIV with the inaugural of 4 March 1825 and closed mid-May; this book resumes the same chapter without opening a new numbered chapter.

Volume VII contains a single body chapter:

  • Chapter XIV (Continued) — The Presidency (15 May 1825 through late May 1828; TOC p. 3–547)

Running page headers preserve month and year (e.g. [May, / 1825.] through 1828.]). OCR is Hathi-style page files (----- PAGE N -----); soft hyphenation and occasional garbled words appear. Front matter includes a short table of contents for the continued chapter only. As in Vol. VI, presidential materials are often minutes of visitors and cabinet business rather than continuous narrative; the editor notes gaps filled later from memoranda.

This CFA Memoirs volume covers the middle of Adams’s presidency: the Georgia–Creek crisis after the murder of General Mackintosh and Governor Troup’s confrontations with federal agents and surveyors; the Panama Congress mission, Senate and House opposition, and the first consolidation of Crawford–Jackson–Calhoun elements against the Administration; Lafayette’s presence and departure on the frigate Brandywine; annual messages stressing internal improvement; the deaths of John Adams and Thomas Jefferson on 4 July 1826 and the mourning processions; British colonial-trade refusal and slave-indemnity compromise; electioneering toward 1828, Jacksonian newspaper wars, and McLean’s ambiguous Post Office; the late tariff fight in the Senate; and closing cabinet debate on missions to Great Britain and Colombia with message No. 12 transmitting three British conventions. Prefer diary date + chapter when building event pages; cite this book as CFA Memoirs Vol. VII.

Opening — May–summer 1825: Creek massacre, Troup, cabinet routine

May 15th, 1825.—Chilly Mackintosh, Colonel Mackintosh, Jim Tallazan, Ben Tallazan. These four Creek Indians called on me this morning before breakfast with a letter from Governor Troup, of Georgia, and a talk sent by him to certain other Creek chiefs… announced to me the murder of the chief called General Mackintosh… It was on Saturday, the 30th of last month, that a party of about four hundred surrounded and set fire to his house, and killed him and another chief… Troup charges Crowell, the Agent, with having instigated this massacre, and vows revenge with a spirit as ferocious as ever inspired any Creek Indian. Adams directs Secretary of War Barbour to confer with the chiefs, measure disposable force for the Georgia border, and draft an answer: the United States cannot interpose in internal feuds of an Indian tribe, but will warn the Upper Creeks against further violence and permit a winter deputation. He apprehends the massacre is only the signal for a ferocious Indian war.

The same week Clay’s October letter to Hammond on objections to Crawford is discussed for publication; Adams doubts its expediency. Indian school and Seneca annuity funds under Monroe and Calhoun require regular accountability. Through summer 1825 the diary mixes Creek and Georgia papers (Gaines councils, Mackintosh party talks, Indian Springs aftermath), army brevet and rank questions, Porter court materials still in train from Vol. VI, and visitors. August brings General Lafayette to Washington: Adams declines private parties on the principle of his predecessors, visits the frigate Brandywine with Lafayette and Southard, and records the General’s leave-taking circuit. Gaps appear where visitor pressure leaves only margin lists of names.

Autumn–winter 1825–26: first annual message, Panama, New Year 1826

Preparation of the annual message fills November. Adams reads Rush the concluding part recommending internal improvement in its full extent; Rush approves though other members of the Administration will not. Sergeant accepts appointment as one of the Ministers to the Congress at Panama; Gallatin declines. Cabinet meeting of 23 November takes Pea-patch fortification claims (Delaware vs. New Jersey), Creek business, and first reading of the message. Forsyth later marks the announced Panama mission as a change of policy; Adams answers that circumstances, not policy, have changed.

December–January bring Edward Thomson’s Philadelphia custom-house failure (near a million dollars in tea duties at risk) and pressure to remove the Collector. Clay urges that the Panama nominations go to the Senate; Adams agrees to send the message on the Monday after adjournment. New Year’s Day 1826 draws two to three thousand visitors; Russian and French Ministers refuse the crowd and ask a separate ceremonial day, which Adams declines. Branch’s hostile Senate resolution and the Panama nomination fight open the session. Lloyd and Mills report the Foreign Relations Committee (Tazewell writing) will report against the mission; confirmation, if it comes, will be by a small majority after long debate.

Winter–spring 1826: Panama as first united opposition; internal improvement

February 1826.—A debate commenced this day in the House of Representatives on two resolutions calling for papers relating to the proposed mission to Panama. This is the first subject upon which a great effort has been made in both Houses to combine the discordant elements of the Crawford and Jackson and Calhoun men into a united opposition against the Administration. The House passes a modified call 124–40. Cabinet and Senate business track the nominations, instructions, and South American correspondence. Internal-improvement language and canal/road papers (including Cumberland Road routing disputes) recur alongside visitor lists and message drafts. Anderson later remarks that the late session produced four or five challenges to duels that ended in smoke, and that a public dinner to John Randolph of Roanoke produced a toast directly instigating assassination.

Summer 1826: Jubilee Fourth; deaths of Jefferson and John Adams

July 4th, 1826—the fiftieth anniversary—Adams joins the Capitol procession with Calhoun following; Joseph Anderson reads the Declaration; Walter Jones delivers the oration; Barbour and Rush solicit subscriptions for Jefferson’s relief with scant immediate success. Within days the mail brings word that John Adams’s end is approaching; Adams determines to leave for Quincy. On the road to Baltimore he learns his father died on the 4th about five in the afternoon. My father had nearly closed the ninety-first year of his life—a life illustrious in the annals of his country and of the world… The time, the manner, the coincidence with the decease of Jefferson, are visible and palpable marks of Divine favor… Funeral honors for Jefferson he leaves to the heads of Departments; his own journey becomes a chain of eulogies and processions (Storrs at Braintree; Sprague at Salem with Webster and Everett present; Wirt’s two-and-a-half-hour discourse on return). After about a hundred days partially released from Washington cares he resumes the stream of visitors and official business.

Autumn 1826–winter 1827: British negotiation; Monroe and Panama/Mexico; Creek surveys

Clay reports Gallatin’s dispatches: the British Government have assumed the new ground of refusing to negotiate upon the Colonial trade, and propose £250,000 sterling to compromise the slave-indemnity question, which Adams authorizes Gallatin to accept after reasonable effort for more. Northwestern boundary under the seventh article of Ghent (Porter and Barclay) also threatens difference. Barbour and Southard sound Monroe on a mission to the Congress of American nations now transferred from Panama to Mexico; Monroe declines on Mrs. Monroe’s health and private affairs. Creek and Georgia survey disputes continue; Indian Springs and Troup language remain live.

January–February 1827 cabinet meetings take the Creek arrest of Georgia surveyors and Troup’s troop of horse ordered to force the survey. The Act of 30 March 1802 (surveying forbidden; military conveyance of trespassers) is consulted. Clay urges force to protect Indian rights; Adams doubts expediency of sending troops against surveyors acting under State authority and prefers civil process and reference to Congress before a conflict of arms. Draft messages avoid the statement that a national–state military clash would dissolve the Union. Messages go to the Senate with Panama papers and land resolutions. Southard reports a scurrilous Jackson letter to him (criticisms at a Fredericksburg dinner) entrusted open to Houston and for the present withheld by Jackson’s friends.

1827–early 1828: electioneering, Jackson papers, McLean, New Year 1828

Through 1827 the diary records newspaper warfare, applications for Jackson court-martial orders on the six militiamen executed for mutiny (Force and Duff Green; Adams reluctant to furnish documents at printers’ request as a measure of hostility), and McLean’s ambiguous Post Office posture—devoted to Calhoun, no friendship to Clay, widely thought hostile to the Administration though he disclaims it. Electioneering language multiplies. January 1st, 1828 opens with a crowded New Year drawing-room; Krudener, Vaughan, Huygens and other ministers attend; Adams signs the release of Edward Thomson from prison and hears McDuffie (Ways and Means) on modifications of the tariff—woollens, hemp, iron, fine cottons, teas, wines. Georgia has nominated Crawford for Vice-President; the prior legislature had already nominated Jackson for the Presidency.

Spring 1828: tariff, canal subscription, cabinet on missions; close of volume

Thomas of Illinois reports the Tariff bill before the Senate: professed protection of domestic manufacturers compounded with taxation peculiarly oppressive upon New England; passage may depend on Massachusetts votes and Webster. Adams declines to interpose to fix Webster’s vote. House passage to third reading of a bill for a one-million-dollar subscription (five annual instalments) to Chesapeake and Ohio Canal stock by a majority of forty-four gratifies the District. Marks of Pennsylvania brings western newspaper tales that Adams’s wife is an Englishwoman; Adams states the facts of the London marriage to Joshua Johnson’s daughter. Barbour and Clay advise removal of McLean after Philadelphia postmaster intrigue; the Retrenchment Committee report is expected as an electioneering pamphlet.

Late May cabinet (Clay, Rush, Southard): whether to nominate ministers of plenipotentiary rank for Great Britain and Colombia now. Adams prefers postponement to the next session; the Cabinet prefer action—Barbour for England, Harrison for Colombia, Porter for War in Barbour’s place. Adams objects that Barbour’s departure would be read as abandonment of the Administration and that an all-northern cabinet (no Southern vote south of the Susquehanna save through Clay’s West) would be pernicious; he floats Gaston of North Carolina and John Williams of Tennessee for War. Concurrent opinion still prefers Porter and, for Colombia, Harrison over Robertson. Adams recalls 1823–24 communications that plenipotentiary rank in the South American States need not continue, and Colombia’s notice that it will keep only a chargé. Meeting adjourns. I sent message to Congress No. 12, with copies of the three Conventions with Great Britain, the ratifications of which were exchanged at London the 2d of last month. END OF VOL. VII.

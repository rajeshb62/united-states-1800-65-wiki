Overview

Rev. Charles A. Goodrich’s A History of the United States of America is a Hartford school and popular history of the early national republic, issued in its third edition in 1823 with engravings. The District of Connecticut copyright notice records deposit on the eighth day of March in the forty-seventh year of Independence under the title “A history of the United States of America, by Rev. Charles A. Goodrich, with engravings.” The work is built not as a continuous unnumbered narrative but as Eleven Periods, each “distinguished by some striking characteristic, or remarkable circumstance,” set out in a GENERAL DIVISION that opens the body and is then carried through running page heads of the form PERIOD … dates … topic.

Period I runs from the discovery of America by Columbus (1492) to the first permanent English settlement at Jamestown (1607) and is distinguished for Discoveries. Period II runs from Jamestown (1607) to the accession of William and Mary (1689) and is distinguished for Settlements of the original states. Period III covers the three colonial wars of King William, Queen Anne, and George II (1689–1756). Period IV is the French and Indian War (1756–1775). Period V is the War of the Revolution (Lexington 1775 to the disbanding of the army at West Point, 1783). Period VI is the formation and establishment of the Federal Constitution (1783–1789). Periods VII–XI are presidential administrations: Washington (1789–1797), Adams (1797–1801), Jefferson (1801–1809), Madison and the late war with Great Britain (1809–1817), and Monroe to “the present time” as of the early 1820s (text page heads PERIOD XI….1817….1822). A printer’s note at the close states that there are 400 pages in the work, plus 24 for plates not included, making 424 pages.

The raw file is continuous Internet Archive OCR (not Hathi leaf markers). Soft-hyphen line breaks and period-head OCR noise (PERIOD iII, PERIOD V1I, &c.) are common; the imprint period structure itself is stable.

Full Period-by-Period Breakdown

Period I — Discoveries (1492–1607)

From Columbus’s discovery through the principal Spanish, French, and English voyages along the eastern coast of North America down to the eve of Jamestown. Goodrich characterizes the period as little more than accounts of expeditions that discovered and more or less explored the principal countries along the Atlantic seaboard. Running heads mark PERIOD I….1492….1607….DISCOVERIES through the early leaves.

Period II — Settlements (1607–1689)

From the settlement of Jamestown to the accession of William and Mary. The text details settlements effected or attempted within the future United States, including wars with the natives, disputes between proprietors and colonies, and the formation of governments, as circumstances that “pertain to, and form a part of, the settlement of new countries.” It embraces the settlement of most of the original states—Massachusetts (including Maine), Connecticut, Rhode Island, New-Hampshire, New-York, New-Jersey, Pennsylvania, Maryland, Delaware, North and South Carolina, and Virginia. Colonial crises such as the Salem witchcraft prosecutions appear in late Period II / early Period III leaves (Cotton Mather’s Magnalia on the afflicted; nineteen executed; special court and release).

Period III — Three colonial wars (1689–1756)

From William and Mary’s accession to England’s declaration of war against France opening the French and Indian War (1756). Because the colonies remained attached to the English crown, they were thrice involved in wars between England and France while the French held Canada and were leagued with powerful Indian tribes; the colonies became the theatre of those operations. Goodrich therefore marks the period as most remarkable for the wars of King William, Queen Anne, and George II.

Period IV — French and Indian War (1756–1775)

From the 1756 declaration of war to the commencement of hostilities by Great Britain against the American colonies in the battle of Lexington (1775). Distinguished for the French and Indian War proper and the run-up to colonial resistance.

Period V — Revolution (1775–1783)

From Lexington (1775) to the disbanding of the American army at West Point, New York (1783). Distinguished for the War of the Revolution. This is the longest stretch of the book by bulk: continuous running heads PERIOD V….1775….1783….REVOLUTION carry campaign and political narrative through the war years to peace and demobilization.

Period VI — Establishment of the Federal Constitution (1783–1789)

From the disbanding of the army (1783) to Washington’s inauguration as President under the Federal Constitution (1789). Distinguished for the formation and establishment of the Federal Constitution. Running heads: PERIOD VI….1783….1789….ESTABLISHMENT OF [the Federal Constitution].

Period VII — Washington’s administration (1789–1797)

From Washington’s inauguration (1789) to John Adams’s inauguration (1797). Distinguished for Washington’s Administration. Early sections stress the inaugural religious language (“my fervent supplications to that Almighty Being…”) and the first business of the new government: revenue by impost and tonnage; executive departments (Jefferson State, Hamilton Treasury, Knox War, Randolph Attorney General); federal judiciary under John Jay with Rutledge, Wilson, Cushing, Harrison, and Blair; support of public credit.

Period VIII — Adams’s administration (1797–1801)

From Adams’s inauguration (1797) to Jefferson’s (1801). Distinguished for Adams’s Administration. The transition from Washington closes free navigation of the Mississippi and New-Orleans deposit, evacuation of British posts, and Mediterranean treaties with Algiers and Tripoli, shaded by French discontent. Section II opens on Washington’s recall of Monroe and despatch of C. C. Pinckney as minister plenipotentiary to adjust differences as France meditated depredations on West India commerce.

Period IX — Jefferson’s administration (1801–1809)

From Jefferson’s inauguration (1801) to Madison’s (1809). Distinguished for Jefferson’s Administration. Opens with Jeffersonian principles—freedom of religion, press, and person under habeas corpus and impartial juries—as the “creed of our political faith.” Section II marks the commencement of the administration by removals from responsible and lucrative offices. The period covers the Louisiana years, commercial restriction, and the approach of non-intercourse and war pressure through the end of Jefferson’s second term (embargo repeal and non-intercourse with France and Britain appear at the Period X hinge).

Period X — Madison’s administration and the late war (1809–1817)

From Madison’s inauguration (1809) to Monroe’s (1817). Distinguished for Madison’s Administration and the late War with Great Britain. Early leaves recount Erskine’s arrangement (orders in council of January and November 1807), the non-intercourse frame of March 1809, and the abrupt close of correspondence with Jackson (Erskine’s successor) for alleged insult. The bulk treats the war years—campaigns, the capture of Washington, and peace—under continuous PERIOD X….1809….1817 heads.

Period XI — Monroe’s administration (1817–1822)

From Monroe’s inauguration (1817) to “the present time” of the third edition (page heads through 1822). Distinguished for Monroe’s Administration. Includes Monroe’s tour examining fortifications and public institutions; the December message on national credit, lake naval reduction with Great Britain, and repeal of internal duties; Mississippi admitted as a sovereign state (11 December in the narrative); suppression of privateer and slave-trade establishments at Galvezton and related islands under Captains Henly and others; and later leaves on Illinois, Arkansas, Alabama, Missouri, and Florida in the index apparatus. Closes with index matter and the printer’s count of 400 text pages + 24 plate pages = 424.

Relationship to the Wiki

Goodrich supplies a single-volume, periodized national narrative written inside the early Monroe years—contemporary to the close of the War of 1812 settlement and the first Missouri-statehood generation—rather than a later multi-volume partisan history. It complements Hildreth’s denser constitutional-era narrative (vols. IV–VI in this batch) as a shorter school text with explicit period boundaries (Washington–Adams–Jefferson–Madison–Monroe) useful for entity and event scaffolding. It pairs with Irving’s western narratives and the Adams Memoirs / LOA Diaries as a popular New England imprint’s framing of the same federal decades.

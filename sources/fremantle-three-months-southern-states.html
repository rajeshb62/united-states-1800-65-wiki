Three Months in the Southern States, April–June 1863

Overview

Arthur James Lyon Fremantle (1835–1901), a Coldstream Guards officer on leave, entered the Confederate States from Mexico in April 1863 and left the Army of Northern Virginia after Gettysburg. The 1863 Blackwood volume is the day-by-day diary he kept from the Atrato / Havana / Matamoros approach through Texas, the trans-Mississippi, Johnston’s Mississippi, Bragg’s Tennessee army, Charleston, Richmond, Lee’s Pennsylvania campaign, and a Northern exit via Hagerstown, Cumberland, Philadelphia, and New York (draft riots of 13–15 July). Portraits in the printed book: Jefferson Davis, Joseph E. Johnston, Leonidas Polk, G. T. Beauregard, James Longstreet, Robert E. Lee.

The Preface states that at the outbreak of the war Fremantle felt “very indifferent as to which side might win,” with a slight English bias toward the North from dislike of slavery; admiration for Southern “gallantry and determination,” and dislike of Northern “foolish bullying,” reversed his feeling. He publishes the diary because the blockade made Southern news arrive mainly through Northern sources. He does not conceal “peculiarities or defects” of Southern people, especially in “the wilder portion of the country,” but concludes that a people of such “unanimity and heroism” is “destined, sooner or later, to become a great and independent nation.”

The extract is clean digital prose from a modern EPUB of the 1863 text (not page OCR), with Blackwood title-page, portrait list, preface, dated diary entries, numbered footnotes, Postscript, and “THE END.” Body structure: Preface + dated diary 2 March–15 July 1863 + Postscript. March entries are the Atlantic/Caribbean approach; the Confederate itinerary proper runs 2 April (Rio Grande) through 9 July (Hagerstown to Hancock). Contemporary names and spellings in the extract (M’Carthy, M’Laws, Mosquite, Petersburgh, Wachita, Kernel, &c.) are preserved as printed.

For this wiki the book is a British officer’s Confederate tour in the campaign summer of 1863: cotton and blockade at Matamoros/Brownsville, Texas society and Sam Houston, the swamp crossing of the Mississippi during Grant’s Vicksburg operations, Johnston at Jackson/Canton, Bragg–Polk–Hardee–Cleburne at Shelbyville/Wartrace, Beauregard/Ripley and Fort Sumter, Jefferson Davis and Judah P. Benjamin at Richmond, Longstreet’s headquarters through Chambersburg and the three days at Gettysburg, and a closing contrast with Northern cities and the New York draft riot.

Preface

At the outbreak of the American war Fremantle, like many countrymen, felt indifferent, with a slight English bias toward the North from dislike of slavery. Admiration for Southern gallantry, and the “unhappy contrast” of Northern “foolish bullying,” produced a “complete revulsion.” Returning to England he found friends hungry for Southern truth: the blockade made intelligence come mainly through Northern sources, and “nowhere is the ignorance of what is passing in the South more profound than it is in the Northern States.” He therefore publishes the diary kept day by day. The Gettysburg portion had already appeared in Blackwood’s Magazine. He has not concealed Southern peculiarities or defects in the wilder country, but thinks no generous man can fail to admire the courage, energy, and patriotism of the whole population and the skill of its leaders “in this struggle against great odds,” and that such unanimity and heroism destine the people “sooner or later, to become a great and independent nation.”

Approach (2 March–1 April 1863)

Leaves England in the royal mail steamer Atrato; St Thomas 17 March; Havana 22 March, where H.M.S. Immortalité (Captain Hancock) takes him and the Irish-Texan merchant M’Carthy toward Matamoros. 1 April: anchored three miles from the mouth of the Rio Grande / Rio Bravo del Norte among about seventy merchant vessels.

Texas border and Matamoros (2–12 April)

2 April. Cutter over the bar (3½ feet, sharks, Orlando recently lost a man) to Bagdad on the Mexican bank: wooden shanties, endless cotton bales, two shallow steamers feeding the outer fleet. Ituria’s buggy toward Brownsville; passless Fremantle taken before Duff’s cavalry officers in flannel shirts, jack-boots, and lone-star hats. Cotton schooner from Galveston: 6 cents a pound there, 36 at the mouth. Meets General Bee (brother of the Bee killed at Manassas) with beef and beer; Bee disavows the “Mongomery” killing. A few miles on, Mongomery’s corpse—slightly buried, rope still on a mesquite: Fremantle’s “first experience of Lynch law within three hours of landing.” Duff’s newly raised regiment: young men, some owning vast tracts and above a hundred slaves; Duff says the affair was wrong but the boys “meant well.” Brownsville ~3,000; ~4,000 troops under Bee.

3–7 April. Crosses to Matamoros (Good Friday; no carriages). Acting British consul Mr Zorn (“Don Pablo”) and partner Behnsen put him up; merchants, Peterhoff owner Bennet elated at capture as a case for the British government. Matamoros ~9,000, consulate riddled in 1861–2 fighting; drought eleven months; diligence robberies with blinds down. Hancock and officers visit; society, gambling, fandango.

8–12 April. Preparations to start inland with M’Carthy; Unionist interlocutor; affectionate leave of the consulate circle.

Interior Texas to Houston (13–30 April)

13–21 April. Breakfast with General Bee; letters for Magruder. Stages and Mexican saddles across mesquite desert: tics, water, horse trouble, lost animals 18 April. Hamlets and ranches; armed travellers.

24–29 April. Push to San Antonio (prettily situated on both banks of the San Antonio River). M’Carthy’s buggy; Colonel Bankhead letters. Cross Guadalupe; exhausted sleep. Alleyton, railroad terminus sprung up in three years, crammed with travellers and cotton speculators; San Antonio–Alleyton 140 miles in forty-six hours.

30 April. First Texan railroad: one class, “all aboard,” jumping on and off, no fences. Richmond on the Brazos—inclined-plane trestle shot; Judge Stockdale vs General Chambers (married soldiers to embrace families once a year). Terry plantation, cotton and corn mixed, more than 200 negroes. Houston at 4.30 p.m., Fannin House; Galveston refugees; theatre; five beds to a room, negro cleaning boots, captain’s lecture on the “genuine African.”

Houston, Galveston, Sam Houston (1–7 May)

1 May. General Scurry (ophthalmia) takes him in; Magruder’s letter. Dinner: Terrill, Pyron, Wharton, Watkins (Sabine Pass), Cook (artillery, ex-U.S. navy, Methodist preacher). Queen’s health; scattered white population as “a sort of aristocracy”; cavalry prefer shot-guns and revolvers to sabres.

2 May. Scurry sends servant John, “an aristocratic negro,” “nearly as white as myself.” In the cars: General Samuel Houston—Virginia-born, U.S. senator at thirty, Tennessee governor at thirty-six, San Jacinto 1836, President of Texas, annexation 1845, deposed as anti-secession governor in 1860; “extremely egotistical and vain.”

4–7 May. Galveston steamer delayed; Huntsville breakfast; Federal prisoners; Crockett; smaller coach at 1.30 a.m. East-Texas stages.

Trans-Mississippi to the river (8–17 May)

8–13 May. Marshall 3 a.m.; stage for Monroe; rough night; General Hebert (Creole, West Pointer) at Monroe. Three negroes from a plantation; row on board; officers and soldiers ~30.

14 May. Mississippi left bank overflowed; Federal gunboats past Vicksburg and Port Hudson; passage only by yawl and skiffs through swamps. Trinity 6 a.m.; Log Bayou; Lake Concordia; alligators and snakes; land at Mr Davis’s; Vidalia opposite Natchez; first bed and boots-off in ten days. Conscripts (Irishman and Pole) dislike the military profession but acknowledge mass enthusiasm.

15–17 May. Cross to Natchez (~6,000, high bluff). Haller Nutt’s pagoda-like house. Hire carriage 100toBrookhaven(66miles)withTexascontractor,woundedMissourianDouglas,soldier′swife;driverNelson,slavepaying4½ a week to work on his own account, “didn’t want to see no Yanks, nor to be no freer than he is.” Farmwomen in “miserable uncertainty,” slaves “generally remain true.” News: Jackson to be evacuated; Johnston assumed command. 16 May: coffee of sweet potatoes; “This is the most unjust war ever waged upon a people by mortal man”; negroes run to swamps; Jackson in enemy hands; farmer’s account of Grierson (~1,500, horses, railroad, paroles, “no barbarities,” Northwestern men). 17 May: Brookhaven full of soldiers; Maxey’s brigade gone to Johnston; Fremantle “always intercepted” (Alexandria, Harrisonburg, now Jackson).

Johnston, Jackson, Vicksburg (18–24 May)

18–20 May. Civilized aspect after the swamp; Bowmont House breakfast; 3 a.m. bombardment (later learned: unsuccessful assault). Jackson wrecked; Johnston concentrating.

21 May. Johnston’s mess (Eustis, Washington). Henderson’s ~50 scouts to Grant’s camps and Pemberton. Yankee surgeon left with wounded at Jackson; Johnston refuses flag-of-truce return, sends him North via Richmond; doctors treated as non-combatants by agreement. Planter Harrold’s slave quarters: clean huts, clothes/shoes short, incurable woman kept thirteen years; Fremantle notes planters shirk the word “slave.” Johnston too weak to attack Grant; will not guess when strong enough. Johnston on titles in a republic; camp-fire hyperbole that nine (Johnston: ninety-nine) in a hundred would rather be Queen Victoria’s subjects than return to the Union—then French/Japanese emperors and even infernal regions as alternatives to “Uncle Abe.”

22 May. Heavy Vicksburg bombardment. Johnston: Confederate combinations wrecked by straggling; qualities (bravery, firearms, patriotism, confidence in Lee/Johnston/Beauregard/Longstreet) vs political officers who will not punish, knapsacks thrown away, early-war going home after a victory. Grant more vigour than expected: crossed below Vicksburg, seized Jackson, invested the fortress; army ~75,000; Vicksburg land defences little valued; garrison ~20,000. Walker to Yazoo City. Canton planter with 700 slaves. Scout: prisoner “surrendered so quick, I couldn’t kill him.” Johnston wounded ten times; senior old-army officer to join; Seven Pines.

23–24 May. Locomotive toward Jackson; Johnston on Stonewall: not a great strategist, unfit perhaps for independent large command, but courage, determination, Providence; indebted to Ewell; flower of Virginians vs Fremont and Banks. Engine wooded from the roadside; Johnston’s Seven Pines wound. Walk last four miles, Johnston carrying cloaks. Loring (one arm) ~6,000 from Crystal Springs; Evans ~3,000 from Charleston; Maxey arriving; Fremantle estimates ~25,000 between Jackson and the Yazoo. Farewell; ambulance; worst railroad; engine off the track. Meridian 7.30 a.m. five hours late; Mobile 7.15 p.m.; engineer had shot a passenger, unhitched, cut the telegraph.

Mobile to Bragg’s army (25 May–5 June)

25–27 May. Mobile disappointing; Montgomery, capital of Alabama.

28–29 May. Chattanooga 4.30 a.m. Walk with Dr Quintard; Polk’s headquarters.

30 May. Prayers; Polk on Murfreesborough (Confederate 30,000 including Breckinridge not engaged first day; losses 10,000 vs 19,000) and Shiloh (Beauregard’s retire order unfortunate; Albert Sidney Johnston’s death). Colonel St Leger Grenfell, Inspector-General of Cavalry: French lancers, Abd-el-Kader, Riff pirates, Turkish contingent, Indian Mutiny, South America, blockade-runner, Morgan’s adjutant; red forage-cap in action; Morgan “enervated by matrimony.” Young Polk on Jackson at Lexington VMI: “be slow to draw the sword in civil strife, but when you draw it, throw away the scabbard.”

31 May. Bishop of Georgia preaches and confirms. Bragg on Murfreesborough: held three and a half days, 6,000 prisoners; Rosecrans “the only man in the Yankee army who was not badly beaten.” Grenfell on Bragg, Polk, Hardee, Cleburne vs “political” generals. Baggage-car to Wartrace with Bragg and the Bishop. Major-General Cleburne: Ballincollig doctor’s son, ran away at 17, three years private/corporal in the British 41st, Arkansas lawyer, only foreigner (with Polignac) at general’s rank; white facings like the 41st. Open-air preaching ~3,000 at Wood’s camp; Bishop Elliott on colonial Church of England and Revolution. Press not gagged: attacks on Davis and generals; Chattanooga Rebel leaked Breckinridge’s departure to Johnston.

1–5 June. Review of Liddell’s Arkansas brigade at Bellbuckle: weak regiments, homespun, officers’ collar bars and branch facings. Touchiness whether Mr Mason was kindly received in England. Outposts with Richmond; leave Shelbyville 6 a.m. 5 June.

Atlanta, Augusta, Charleston (6–15 June)

6–7 June. Atlanta 3 a.m.; Augusta (~20,000).

8 June. Charleston 5 a.m. Beauregard absent in Florida. General Ripley (Northern-born “red-hot” rebel, Enfield connection, artillery reputation) commands forts. Merchant Robertson: Combahee raid by Northern armed negroes, Blake/Heyward losses, city shops shut, fire wilderness a mile by half a mile, blockade-running speculation, slave-auction ads, hotels crowded, $8 a day.

9 June. Captain Feilden (late 42d Highlanders) on Beauregard’s staff. Boat to Fort Sumter: pentagon, two casemate tiers plus barbette, 68 guns (10-inch columbiads 128-lb shot, Brooke/Blakely, Parrott, Dahlgren, 11-inch from Keokuk). Garrison 350 regulars under Colonel Rhett, many foreigners. Ironclad attack eight weeks earlier: 2h25m, one man killed (flagstaff), Keokuk disabled in a quarter hour and sank next morning, none closer than 900 yards. Harbour: Pinckney, Ripley, Moultrie/Bee/Beauregard on Sullivan’s, Johnson, Cummins Point, Wagner on Morris; bar nine miles, thirteen blockaders; Folly Island Yankees. Blockade-runners painted water-colour; Kate double-screw; Feilden shows new ironclads vs Chicora and Palmetto State. Ripley “blockade dinner.”

10–15 June. Exchange office; Colonel Rice; St Michael’s; goodbye to Beauregard; Wilmington 16 June.

Richmond (16–19 June)

17 June. Petersburg 3 a.m. Jefferson Davis via Benjamin: silent despair vs noisy grief; Grenfell’s quarrel with civil power; Confederate generals must keep popularity; black-flag talk vs men who have thrown down arms; Lord Russell and neutrality (Davis: open ports to prizes of both); Davis’s wish to have joined the army; West Point / Secretary of War as the start in generals. Winchester recaptured by Ewell; hope Milroy not taken alive. Favourable impression of Davis’s manners.

18 June. Seddon, Secretary of War: letters for Lee and Longstreet. Aides Wood, Lee, Johnston (sons of R. E. Lee and A. S. Johnston). Capitol librarian Thompson; captured silk Union colours vs Confederate bunting; 37th New York colours twice, second inscribed “Fairoaks.” Mrs S—— converted by Lincoln’s 75,000-man call. Butler King: ruin borne equably; South looks to English merchants and ships after the war.

19 June. Drewry’s Bluff: Monitor and Galena repulse; Yorktown/Patrick Henry; ironclad Richmond; 7-inch Brooke. Beauregard’s view: ironclads cannot stand plunging fire even of smooth-bores. Deserter to be shot at Chaffin’s Bluff. Johnston’s “inactivity” in Mississippi; Vicksburg given up in conversation; papers as violent as New York’s; press liberty “carried to its fullest extent.”

To Lee and Chambersburg (20–30 June)

20–27 June. Train to Culpeper; captured Chancellorsville rifles rotting; Stoneman’s raid. Sergeant Norris (Maryland gentleman now a private) rides him toward Lawley in the rain, sore-backed horses. Army already advanced after Winchester. Lawley ill; Greenwood/Chambersburg approaches. Hood introduced (33, Texan/Alabama idol). Moses requisitions three days’ rations; corporation hides keys; Lee forbids searching private houses; Union women abuse Moses.

28–29 June. No one under general into Chambersburg without Lee’s pass. Pennsylvanian women assail Fremantle until gold appears; “Yankee” a reproach there too. Lee’s non-retaliation order; troops’ forbearance vs Southern towns Fremantle has seen ravaged; “Pennsylvanian Dutch” unpatriotic, abuse Lincoln. Longstreet on Texas and polite sentries. Pendleton (artillery chief, Episcopal clergyman, surplice over uniform); Pickett (ringlets, San Juan Island).

30 June. Longstreet introduces Lee: 56, “almost without exception the handsomest man of his age I ever saw,” courteous, three stars, no arms, grey jacket, never sleeps in a house; relations with Longstreet “quite touching”; Jackson the third simple servant of the country until death. Texans destroy Chambersburg whisky under orders. News: Hooker replaced by Meade. Battle expected on this road, not Harrisburg; Ewell ordered to reunite; Stuart’s cavalry valued for raids not for charging infantry.

Gettysburg (1–4 July)

1 July. Hill’s corps passes; Stonewall Brigade (mostly Virginians) run ahead to look at Longstreet; Florida brigade: “here’s the old bulldog again.” Wounded and prisoners; Pennsylvanian colour captured. 4.30 p.m. with Lee and Hill: enemy driven to cemetery ridge; Hill unwell, two divisions in, Yankees fought with unusual determination, colour-bearer retiring last. Ewell 3.30 on the enemy’s right; Reynolds reported killed. Town occupied; ~6,000 prisoners, 10 guns; 1st and 11th Corps. Longstreet: position “very formidable,” will be entrenched. Footnote: fight came off prematurely; neither Lee nor Longstreet intended it that day.

2 July. 3.30 a.m.; Lee, Hill, Longstreet, Hood, Heth (head wound) consulting and whittling. Semicircle five to six miles; Ewell left (cupola), Hill centre, Longstreet right; only McLaws and Hood (Pickett still rear). Dead silence till 4.45 p.m.

3 July. Cannonade then Pickett (under 5,000, two brigades absent) with Heth and Pettigrew to cross a mile of open ground. Fremantle and the Austrian fail to reach Ewell’s cupola under crossfire. 2.30: stream of wounded “as great as the crowd in Oxford Street.” Longstreet on a snake fence, calm: “we’ve attacked and been repulsed: look there!” Pickett had carried the position ~20 minutes then retired when left gave way. Lee “perfectly sublime,” rallying alone: “This has been a sad day for us, Colonel”; to Willcox, “all this has been my fault—it is I that have lost this fight.” Garnett and Armistead killed, Kemper mortally; Pickett nearly all field officers hit; slaughter ~one mile square, one hour. Gunners still confident in “Uncle Robert.” Johnson said successful on the left; Hood’s Texans and cavalry. First food in fifteen hours at 10 p.m.

4 July. Moses’s trunk stolen by stragglers. Flag of truce: Federals think Longstreet wounded and a prisoner—he sends thanks. Pendleton: 12-pounder Napoleon preferred; almost all artillery captured or recast from old 6-pounders. Army to move toward Virginia that evening for want of ammunition; hoped the enemy might attack on the Fourth.

Retreat, Hagerstown, North, New York (5–15 July)

Crosses toward the Potomac; Hagerstown. 9 July: to Hancock; detained; explains himself to General Kelly as a British officer who entered via Mexico to break no blockade, Longstreet’s guest, no letters; Kelly lets him go, keeps the driver, who then talks. Farmer supper, violent Unionists. 10–12 July: Cumberland, Johnstown militia garlands, Philadelphia Confederate prisoners (one suggestion they be drowned), New York Fifth Avenue Hotel 10 p.m. 13 July: Broadway prosperity, 550bounties,greybackposters;shopsclosing;fire,mob,negrochased,"Downwiththeb——ynigger";citizencavalrywhocannotsithorses.∗∗14July:∗∗Irishwaitersdrafted;NortherntalkofRebeldemoralisationvsLee′sarmyfourdaysearlier"asfulloffightasever";Gettysburg+Vicksburg+PortHudsonhave"turnedeverybody′shead";riot:telegraphscut,trackstorn,draftsuspended,300 commutation hated. 15 July: military in the hotel; boarding the China.

Postscript

On the China, upper-class Northerners still ignorant of Southern feeling; illusions of Charleston/Mobile/Savannah/Wilmington about to fall, Lee and Bragg wrecked, Mississippi open. Fremantle on Confederate recruiting: no discharge however wounded; maimed to public labour; boys reserved until fit; negroes “a last resort” not soon contemplated. Arms: Augusta powder, Atlanta caps, Tredegar cannon, Federal generals as “indefatigable purveyors of artillery.” Northern gentlemen determined to crush the rebellion but not to fight themselves. Quotes Polk: “How can you subjugate such a people as this?” Cannot believe the nineteenth century will witness destruction of “such a gallant race.”

Notes on use

  • Lens: British professional officer, increasingly Confederate-sympathetic; slavery described as he met it (planter language, hiring-out, Combahee raid, New York riot hatred of negroes) without later moral frame. Independence forecast is the book’s own closing politics.
  • Gettysburg: eyewitness from Longstreet’s staff axis, not a complete tactical survey; 2 July fighting is mostly the morning wait; 3 July is the aftermath of Pickett as seen from Longstreet then Lee.
  • Names: Jefferson Davis, Lee, Longstreet, Johnston, Bragg, Polk, Beauregard, Ripley, Seddon, Benjamin, Hood, Pickett, Cleburne, Grenfell, Sam Houston, Magruder, Bee, Scurry.
